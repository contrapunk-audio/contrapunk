# Research: Preset 25 — Bebop Chase (performer/HCI)

## Summary

**Bebop Chase should be sold as a bounded delayed-answer exercise, not as a Parker/Gillespie simulator.** The documented intersection is clean, metrically grounded melodic invention, including unison statements and exchanged phrases: the 1945 “Ko Ko” recording begins in unison and then has Parker and Gillespie trade eight-bar phrases, while Parker’s mature phrasing varies phrase placement and length against a stable form. [NPR](https://text.npr.org/1081208) [Love, MTO §§2–3](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.love.html)

The present Canon/“Free Imitation” lane is note-triggered. It can delay, transpose, and time-compress every attack and release, but it cannot hear a completed four-to-eight-note phrase, choose its salient portion, or answer with fewer notes. Therefore the player—not the preset—must create concise calls and rests; “shortened answer” is only truthful if it means a **time-compressed complete echo**, not a truncated phrase.

## Findings

### 1. Referenced intersection, distinct roles, and generic bebop

1. **Shared Parker/Gillespie practice represented here (high confidence):** metrically exact, cleanly articulated lines; unison coordination; then separated turns or phrase exchange over a known form. “Ko Ko” is unusually direct evidence: alto and trumpet open together, exchange eight-bar phrases, return briefly to unison, and then yield to Parker’s solo. Parker himself described his ideal as “very clean, very precise,” while the account stresses coherent melodic-rhythmic ideas rather than undifferentiated speed. [NPR](https://text.npr.org/1081208) The Library of Congress National Recording Registry essay likewise documents the 1945 session and Parker’s use of upper chord extensions, rhythmic displacement, and melodic logic. [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/ko-ko.pdf)
2. **Parker’s role/style in this preset (high confidence):** use breath-bounded but metrically mobile melodic sentences, vary long and short spans, and connect tension to resolution across the barline. Love’s 156-chorus corpus finds several recurring phrase organizations rather than one square template; rests define phrase boundaries, and familiar melodic paths are truncated, extended, divided, rhythmically displaced, or chromatically inflected. This supports “develop and vary a compact idea,” not “run a chromatic scale fast.” [Love, MTO §§3.2–3.15, 4.3–4.16](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.love.html)
3. **Gillespie’s distinct role/style in this preset (medium-high confidence):** project a more declarative, trumpet-like reply and an arranger’s awareness of role and space, rather than treating him as a second Parker line. Gillespie was a trumpeter, composer, arranger, and bandleader central to the 1940s development of bebop; on “Ko Ko” he also moved from the trumpet introduction to piano accompaniment behind Parker. [Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/object/npg_NPG.93.475) [NPR](https://text.npr.org/1081208) In oral history he discusses the collective development of altered harmony, including flattened fifths and phrases heard across keys; this is evidence of harmonic conception, not permission for random chromatic saturation. [Howard University Jazz Oral History Project](https://dh.howard.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1006&context=hujohp_transcripts)
4. **Generic bebop, not uniquely Parker/Gillespie:** small-group texture, swing subdivision, syncopation, chromatic approaches, upper chord tones, ii–V motion, and learned formulae belong to a broader idiom. The preset’s identity should come from **clean call → delayed compressed pursuit → space**, with Parker/Gillespie named as historical references, not from claiming ownership of every generic bebop device.
5. **Interaction is not constant copying (high confidence):** scholarship distinguishes microinteraction (timing), macrointeraction (shared intensity), and occasional motivic response. It explicitly warns that motivic imitation is intermittent, and cites Parker’s rhythm section holding the form rather than following his metric displacement. [Givan, MTO §§5, 7, 10–16](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.givan.html) Thus a machine that echoes every note is a useful practice foil but a narrower interaction than a human partner.

### 2. Performer contract

#### Best source gesture

- **Use:** one clean, monophonic **four-to-eight-note burst**, ideally one beat to two bars long, with a memorable rhythm, mixed steps and one controlled leap, and a definite final pitch.
- **Then:** stop. Leave at least the configured delay plus roughly one beat so the answer can become the foreground.
- **Do not require:** a borrowed melody, stock lick, chord-scale run, or famous recording quotation. Original scale-degree shapes such as `3–5–4–2 | 1` or `1–2–♭3–3 | 5–2` are sufficient; rhythm and closure matter more than chromatic note count.

#### Register

- **Clean monophonic guitar:** center calls around MIDI **52–76 (E3–E5)**. This keeps ordinary positions available, leaves headroom for an upward answer, and avoids the least stable low-string pitch-to-MIDI region and cramped extreme high register. If the answer is transposed upward, begin nearer MIDI 52–69.
- **Keyboard:** use a horn-like right-hand register, roughly MIDI **55–84 (G3–C6)**. Keep the source in one hand and one register at first; use register contrast only between phrases.
- These are interaction recommendations, not claims about the full historical ranges of alto saxophone or trumpet.

#### Tempo range and precision

- **Recommended working range:** **140–220 BPM in 4/4**. Begin at 140–170; raise tempo only when attacks, releases, and rests remain intentional. Love’s Parker corpus uses ≥160 BPM and notes that slower performances change surface subdivision, but speed is not the identity. [Love §1.4 and n.6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.love.html)
- **Transport is required** for a beat-valued delay and time ratio to be predictable. Use an audible count-in/metronome. Land the start of each call intentionally on a beat or upbeat; internal syncopation may cross beats, but do not drift unknowingly.
- The rhythm section/form must remain mentally fixed even when the phrase sounds displaced; Parker’s bandmates were specifically advised not to chase his displaced beat. [Givan §5](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.givan.html)

#### Note length, articulation, dynamics, density, and silence

- **Note lengths:** mostly 35–70% of the notated inter-onset span; allow the destination note to reach 70–100%. Avoid both continuous legato overlap and uniform pinprick staccato.
- **Articulation:** clear fronts, lightly detached interior notes, one or two purposeful accents, and a clean release. Alternate-picked guitar or separate keyboard attacks are safer than smeared slides, bends, hammer-on flurries, or pedal blur.
- **Dynamics:** medium (`mp–mf`) for the first calls; one later call may rise to `f`, then withdraw. The current lane copies input velocity per echoed attack; it does not compose a new dynamic contour.
- **Density:** no more than eight source notes before listening; target four-to-six. At 140–180 BPM, favor eighth-note motion with a rest or held endpoint rather than uninterrupted sixteenths.
- **Silence:** leave one to two beats after a short call, and longer if the configured answer delay requires it. Every two or three exchanges, leave a full bar. Love treats inter-onset gaps/rests as constitutive phrase boundaries, not empty waste. [Love §§3.2–3.4](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.love.html)

#### Phrase and section development

Use a simple 8- or 12-bar practice arc:

1. **Establish (calls 1–2):** four notes, mid-register, one rhythm, `mp`.
2. **Vary (calls 3–4):** retain the rhythm but change one interval or shift the entrance by an eighth note.
3. **Build (call 5):** six-to-eight notes, slightly higher and louder; one chromatic approach only if it resolves clearly.
4. **Thin (call 6):** answer the machine with a two-to-four-note human comment, not another long run.
5. **Close:** held destination, then a full bar of silence; stop/release and verify cleanup.

This reflects Parker’s practice of recurring paths with changed phrasing, timing, truncation, and extension, rather than literal repetition. [Love §§4.9–5.6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.love.html)

#### Listening/responding instruction

After releasing the final source note, **wait until the delayed layer reaches its final attack or release**. Hear whether its rhythm remains recognizable after compression. On the next turn, respond to **one** property only—rhythm, contour, final pitch, or register. Do not play continuously over it. This creates alternating roles and makes the machine limitation musically useful.

### 3. Instrument-specific limits and opportunities

#### Clean-monophonic guitar

- Use one picked note at a time; mute adjacent strings. Avoid ringing open strings, dyads, rake attacks, artificial harmonics, wide bends, heavy vibrato during pitch detection, and legato ornaments that do not yield reliable NoteOn/NoteOff pairs.
- Retrigger repeated pitches cleanly. Release before the next source call so ownership and phrase space stay audible.
- Keep bursts physically modest: one position shift or one string crossing pattern. Givan’s transcription-based comparison observes that fast intricate chromatic figuration is harder to replicate immediately on guitar; Wes Montgomery answered a keyboard’s chromatic passage with a diatonicized contour instead. That is an authoritative precedent for preserving shape rather than forcing every chromatic detail. [Givan §§14–15](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.16.22.3/mto.16.22.3.givan.html)

#### Keyboard opportunities and chord-density limits

- Keyboard can execute crisp repeated notes, broader leaps, exact release timing, and deliberate register contrast more reliably than guitar-to-MIDI.
- Begin with right-hand monophony. A left-hand shell may be played only if it is routed outside the preset’s source lane; otherwise every chord tone becomes another independent trigger.
- **Source-lane chord limit: one simultaneous note.** If polyphonic input cannot be separated by routing, omit chords and sustain pedal entirely. Even two- or three-note voicings multiply pending answers and, because the Canon voice may itself emit a harmony stack, can produce six or more sounding notes from one gesture.

### 4. Failure gestures

- Continuous eighths/sixteenths with no breath: every note schedules an answer, producing a note storm rather than a chase.
- Chords, sustain pedal, or guitar string bleed: each detected pitch becomes a separate delayed event.
- Releasing short notes under `Cancel` before the delay: pending answers can disappear, making the preset seem broken.
- Holding many notes under `Forever`, or stacking new calls before old releases: overlapping owners and mud.
- Rubato or stopped transport: beat-based pending events do not advance predictably; the phrase may appear frozen.
- Extreme low guitar notes, bends, slides, and ghost attacks: unreliable pitch/onset data become faithfully repeated errors.
- Random chromaticism, maximum speed, uniformly accented notes, or uninterrupted high-register playing: a caricature contradicted by Parker’s varied phrase spans and emphasis on clarity/coherence. [NPR](https://text.npr.org/1081208) [Love](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.love.html)

### 5. What the current Free Imitation/Canon layer can and cannot manufacture

Inspection target: `crates/contrapunk-companion/src/canon_lane.rs`.

**It can:**

- schedule an answer for **each incoming NoteOn**, once per configured Canon voice;
- delay in transport beats, diatonically transpose, and scale phrase-relative onset timing with `time_ratio`;
- preserve or proportionally scale each source note’s duration from the matching NoteOff;
- preserve source velocity and channel;
- optionally emit a per-note harmony stack, not merely one imitating pitch;
- reset its sequence anchor after more than two beats between input events; and
- apply voice → lane → global Hold behavior (`Cancel`, `NearFuture`, `PhraseEnd`, `Forever`).

**It cannot:**

- wait for a completed four-to-eight-note call before answering;
- infer phrase identity or musical salience;
- detect a phrase boundary in the musical sense (the two-beat silence threshold only resets the time-scaling anchor);
- shorten a phrase by dropping its opening, middle, or tail;
- select, vary, invert, recombine, or cadence a motif;
- decide that some source notes are ornaments and should not be copied;
- swing, accent, humanize, or create a contrasting dynamic contour independently; or
- listen back to the player and alter a later answer responsively.

**Crucial wording:** with `time_ratio < 1`, it can make the **entire echo shorter in elapsed time**. It still repeats every scheduled attack. Calling that a “shortened phrase” without qualification would falsely imply phrase recognition/truncation. Also, because `compute_voice_stack` may harmonize each subject, a default multi-pitch configuration is not literally a single pursued horn line.

### 6. Product copy

**Proposed Result**

> Clean single-note bursts return as delayed, time-compressed pursuits. Inspired by the turn-taking and precision heard in Parker/Gillespie performances; this is a note-for-note arrangement echo, not a reconstruction of either improviser.

**Required approximation disclosure**

> The current layer repeats every detected note after a beat-based delay. It does not recognize a phrase or choose a shorter motif, so your rests create the call-and-response shape.

**Concise Play it like**

> Play four-to-eight crisp single notes, stop, and let the compressed echo finish before answering it.

**Expanded Play it like**

> Use a clean mid-register line in 4/4: four-to-eight notes, clear attacks, one accented turn, and a definite ending. Leave enough space to hear the complete delayed echo. On the next call, keep the rhythm but alter one interval, entrance, or final note. Build once by moving slightly higher or louder, then end with a held note and a full-bar rest. Guitar: mute unused strings and avoid bends or ringing dyads. Keyboard: use one hand, no sustain pedal, and send no chords into this lane.

### 7. 30–60 second acceptance exercise

At **160 BPM, 4/4**, transport running, configure one audible delayed voice with a clearly sub-unity time ratio and use a monophonic sound.

1. **0–10 s:** play four eighth-note attacks in mid-register, release cleanly, then rest. Confirm exactly four delayed attacks occur in the same order and finish sooner than the source span.
2. **10–25 s:** repeat the rhythm with one changed interval and a higher final note; wait through the full answer. Confirm there is no answer until the configured beat delay and no omitted/source-added attack.
3. **25–40 s:** play a six-note call with one accented pickup and held destination; listen through its release. Confirm velocity follows the source rather than acquiring a new machine accent pattern.
4. **40–50 s:** play a two-note closing tag, release, leave one full bar, then stop/disable or Panic. Confirm zero sounding and pending notes.

**Pass:** the user can hear alternating call/pursuit, count every source attack in the answer, distinguish time compression from phrase truncation, and end cleanly. **Fail:** any chord/extra onset, missing matched release, emission while stopped that violates the transport contract, or residue after disable/Panic.

### 8. Lifecycle expectations

- Applying/replacing the preset should Panic once and reset lane runtime before the new snapshot becomes active.
- Disable and `reset_runtime` must clear pending NoteOns, pending NoteOffs, held-source ownership, and phrase anchor; already-dispatched notes still require the surface’s normal all-notes-off path.
- `Cancel` should remove not-yet-fired attacks and release already-fired notes; `NearFuture` and `PhraseEnd` must bound tails; `Forever` preserves scheduled material but still must clear on disable/Panic/reconfigure.
- With stopped transport, no beat-dependent answer should silently mature; resume should be deterministic, and stop/disable/Panic must not leave a note hanging.
- Reconfigure/voice replacement must drop in-flight queues deterministically. Acceptance should finish with zero active and pending notes on every supported surface.

## Confidence and gaps

- **High confidence:** “Ko Ko” unison/trading structure; Parker’s varied phrase organization; the distinction between motivic interaction and constant echo; current Canon note-by-note scheduling, duration scaling, phrase-anchor reset, and Hold semantics.
- **Medium-high confidence:** the recommended gesture, register, tempo, articulation, and interaction sequence. These are HCI translations constrained by clean guitar/keyboard use, not historical transcriptions.
- **Medium confidence:** mapping Gillespie to the more declarative/arranger-aware response role. His composer-arranger-bandleader and multi-instrument function are documented, but assigning one machine voice “the Gillespie role” remains a product metaphor.
- **Gap:** no controlled user test yet establishes the fastest reliable clean-guitar tempo or safest MIDI register across pickups/interfaces. Calibrate with actual onset/pitch telemetry before narrowing the public range.
- **Gap:** exact preset-25 lane values were not present in the accepted catalog entry; synthesis must verify delay, `time_ratio`, transpose, `voice_count`, Hold, mix, and surface capability before activation.
- **Gap:** true phrase truncation requires the planned phrase/motif Lane. Until then, retain the approximation disclosure and do not promise “shortened phrases.”

## Gaps

A phrase-aware implementation is the only honest route to the catalog’s literal “shortened delayed answers.” Until that capability exists, the operational preset should expose a time-compressed note-for-note pursuit, require monophonic burst-and-rest input, and use the Result/approximation wording above.
