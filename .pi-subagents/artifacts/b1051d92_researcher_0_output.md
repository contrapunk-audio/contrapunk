# HISTORY / PRIMARY-SOURCE RESEARCH — Preset 27 “Quartal Colossus”

## Summary

A defensible “Quartal Colossus” reference is not “all McCoy Tyner” and not simply “stack fourths over a minor pentatonic scale.” It is a bounded arrangement study of Tyner’s forceful, open, rhythmically active piano language as documented in the John Coltrane Quartet of roughly 1960–65 and consolidated in selected leader recordings from *The Real McCoy* (1967) through the early Milestone period, especially *Sahara* (1972). Tyner’s own testimony stresses openness, resolution, listening, contrast, and development: fourth-based sonorities are a means of keeping harmony mobile, while power arises from responsive ensemble motion rather than constant loudness.

The product should therefore generate an **interactive, modal/open-voicing arrangement behavior**, not claim to reproduce Tyner, his touch, or any named recording. Its historical model should move from an audible tonal anchor into increasingly mobile, rhythmically forceful voicings, then thin or lay out and return; it must preserve space for the live player and avoid treating Coltrane’s compositions, Elvin Jones’s rhythmic field, or the quartet’s collective achievements as Tyner’s alone.

---

## 1. Scope and corpus

### Referenced period

**Primary target: 1960–72, in two connected but distinct settings.**

1. **John Coltrane Quartet, 1960–65:** selected recordings where Tyner’s open, fourth-rich accompaniment is inseparable from a stable tonal/modal field and from interaction with Coltrane, Jimmy Garrison, and Elvin Jones: *My Favorite Things* (1960), *Africa/Brass* (1961), *Live at the Village Vanguard* (1961), *Coltrane* (1962), *Live at Birdland* (1963), *Crescent* (1964), and *A Love Supreme* (1964). This is the clearest source for the preset’s “forceful modal support” premise, but it is ensemble evidence, not solo-authored evidence.
2. **Tyner as leader, 1962–72:** *Inception* and *Reaching Fourth* (both recorded in 1962) establish that his own early albums mixed hard-bop, standards, blues, and modal procedures; *The Real McCoy* (recorded April 21, 1967) provides the strongest compact leader statement; *Extensions* and *Asante* (1970) broaden instrumentation and foreground African-oriented rhythmic/cultural concerns; *Sahara* (1972) marks the early Milestone breakthrough and a more expansive leader identity. The official Tyner discography confirms this sequence rather than implying one unchanging career-long setting. [McCoy Tyner official discography](https://mccoytyner.com/discography/)

### Comparison corpus, not the preset’s center

Later solo, trio, big-band, Latin, orchestral, popular-song, and guitar projects are retained as evidence against an essentialist “one trick” portrait. The NEA notes Tyner’s later exploration of trio personnel, a singer, 1990s big band, Latin American forms, and Burt Bacharach repertoire, while retaining acoustic piano. [National Endowment for the Arts](https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/mccoy-tyner) His 2008 interview around *Guitars* also describes deliberate contrast among solo, quartet, quintet, sextet, big-band, string, and other settings. [Dan Tepfer interview](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)

### Evidentiary boundary

The corpus supports a preset about **open harmonic mobility, strong rhythmic punctuation, modal/tonal anchoring, and responsive density**. It does **not** support a claim that every Tyner performance is modal, every voicing is quartal, or later projects merely intensify the 1960s quartet formula. Gregory Satterthwaite’s scholarly study was explicitly written to correct that reduction: the 1962–63 leader recordings balance bebop syntax, pentatonic melody, quartal harmony, and mainstream practices. [Satterthwaite, *Beyond Fourths and Pentatonics*](https://doi.org/10.12794/metadc1703303)

## 2. Historical and aesthetic context

Tyner’s language joined several traditions rather than emerging as a detachable chord recipe. He named Bud Powell and Thelonious Monk as central early inspirations, studied European classical repertory and theory, played R&B, blues, shuffle/“house rockin’,” and encountered African drumming and dance as a teenager in Philadelphia. In his later account, these were not mutually exclusive influences but a “multitude of exposures.” [Dan Tepfer interview](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842) The Smithsonian oral-history transcript independently documents his Philadelphia upbringing, theory/harmony study, formative musicians, and Coltrane years. [Smithsonian Jazz Oral History, 2011 transcript](https://americanhistory.si.edu/sites/default/files/file-uploader/Tyner-McCoy-Transcription-2020.pdf)

The practical musical problem was how to support extended improvisation without either over-specifying conventional functional changes or dissolving all orientation. Tyner later described a vamp/modal feeling as freedom within an “open sound,” contrasting it with the restriction of densely changing bebop harmony—while also saying he disliked reducing his practice to the label “modal.” [Jazz Resource Center interview, 2005](http://www.jazzcenter.org/tyner/interview_2005.htm) In 2008 he was more specific: once the root of a sound is established, he keeps voicings open so they can move; apparently distant sounds can connect if they are voiced and **resolved** convincingly. [Dan Tepfer interview](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)

That balance—anchored but not locked, mobile but not arbitrary—is more historically defensible than “quartal harmony equals ambiguity.” Tyner’s fourth-rich sound had precedents in classical and jazz practice, and he acknowledged influence rather than claiming invention. What became distinctive was the integrated result: voicing, touch, rhythm, pedal control, blues grounding, form, and ensemble timing. Pianist Ethan Iverson’s historically sourced essay usefully notes that fourth chords predated Tyner while arguing that his application transformed jazz-piano practice; it also reproduces Tyner’s 1963 account of discipline, spontaneity, and accompaniment. [Ethan Iverson, “McCoy Tyner’s Revolution”](https://ethaniverson.com/mccoy-tyners-revolution/)

## 3. Career and style evolution

### A. Formation and early professional work: 1950s–60

Tyner’s formation included classical technique/theory, blues and dance music, Philadelphia jam sessions, Powell/Monk influence, the Jazztet, and early contact with Coltrane. This matters because his later vocabulary retained song form, blues, swing, and functional harmonic knowledge. The preset should not pretend that open fourths replaced this foundation.

### B. Coltrane Quartet: 1960–65

The quartet supplied a long-running laboratory. Tyner said Coltrane was the leader and conceptual spearhead, but the band shaped the music collectively: Coltrane listened to the rhythm section, and ideas passed in both directions. [JazzWeekly interview](https://www.jazzweekly.com/interviews/tyner.htm) In a separate recollection, Tyner said the group could make a song around a scale, had a set tonality without a rigid form, and reached *A Love Supreme* as a culmination of years of communication rather than as an isolated miracle. [Jerry Jazz Musician interview, 2001](https://www.jerryjazzmusician.com/mccoy-tyner-talks-about-john-coltrane-and-the-recording-of-a-love-supreme/)

The evolution here is temporal and relational:

- **Within phrases:** Tyner might add a rhythmic/harmonic “push,” suggest a new idea, or lay out completely. In his 1963 account, withholding piano gave the soloist fewer harmonic boundaries; the foundation and “point of return” nevertheless remained understood. [Iverson’s reproduction of Tyner’s 1963 DownBeat account](https://ethaniverson.com/mccoy-tyners-revolution/)
- **Across a performance:** an initial tonality, vamp, or theme could support successive plateaus of greater rhythmic and harmonic freedom, followed by renewed orientation. Tyner described *A Love Supreme* as a plateau built from earlier experimentation and used such plateaus as launching points for the next idea. [JazzWeekly interview](https://www.jazzweekly.com/interviews/tyner.htm)
- **Across the project:** the quartet’s mature sound was not Tyner accompanying in isolation. Garrison supplied grounded time/ostinato, Jones created a powerful but listening polyrhythmic field, Coltrane led repertoire and direction, and Tyner’s open voicings and rhythmic attacks supplied both foundation and provocation. Tyner recalled that Jones could switch to brushes for piano solos and respond complementarily to Coltrane’s phrases; force was dynamically regulated, not indiscriminate. [Dan Tepfer interview](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)

By 1965 Coltrane’s music was moving toward a denser, freer ensemble language. The preset should not treat the later Coltrane direction as merely “more Tyner.” Tyner’s departure marks a real boundary: his preference for melody, audible piano, and coherent resolution did not map automatically onto every subsequent Coltrane texture.

### C. Early leader records: 1962–64

Tyner’s simultaneous Impulse! trio and small-group records reveal a wider practice than the quartet stereotype. *Inception* moves among hard-bop language, Coltrane-era modality, standards, and blues; *Reaching Fourth* creatively reharmonizes standards. Satterthwaite’s analysis makes this period the strongest counter-evidence to a fourths-only caricature. [Satterthwaite](https://doi.org/10.12794/metadc1703303) Iverson’s survey likewise hears modal language in “Effendi” beside Red Garland-like standard playing and a recurring blues commitment. [Iverson](https://ethaniverson.com/mccoy-tyners-revolution/)

### D. Post-Coltrane consolidation: 1966–69

*The Real McCoy* is the preset’s most defensible leader anchor because it preserves open harmony and force while proving Tyner’s independent compositional/formal agency. Blue Note describes five Tyner originals with distinct internal logics: the rhythmic upheaval of “Passion Dance,” the slower and reflective “Search for Peace” and “Contemplation,” polyrhythmic “Four by Five,” and the relaxed “Blues on the Corner.” [Blue Note, “The Real McCoy”](https://www.bluenote.com/spotlight/the-real-mccoy/)

That one album defeats an always-maximal implementation. It begins forcefully but includes sustained reflection, different tempos, blues release, and tightly focused forms. “Passion Dance” also demonstrates that force is distributed among Tyner, Jones, Henderson, and Carter. Calling all of its rhythmic character “Tyner style” would erase the ensemble.

### E. Broader cultural and ensemble palette: 1970–late 1970s

*Extensions* and *Asante* bring African-oriented identity and percussion more explicitly forward; *Sahara* launches the commercially and artistically prominent Milestone era. Tyner connected projects such as *Asante* to teenage exposure to African dance and rhythm, but described this as cultural identification and lived contact, not an exotic decorative scale. [Dan Tepfer interview](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842) Blue Note’s archive describes *Extensions* as a widening of scope and *Asante* as placing African inspiration in a swirling percussive bed. [Blue Note Tyner catalogue](https://www.bluenote.com/artist/mccoy-tyner/)

The early 1970s therefore justify greater expansiveness, percussion-conscious momentum, and leader-directed forms—but not the claim that “African” means generic drums, static ostinato, or maximal volume. Those would be cultural and musical caricatures.

### F. Later career: 1980s–2000s

Tyner continued with trios while also using singers, big band, Latin ensembles, orchestra, popular-song projects, and guitars. He said he deliberately changed settings for contrast and reinterpreted old compositions because his relationship to them changed over time. [JazzWeekly interview](https://www.jazzweekly.com/interviews/tyner.htm) He also said tunes often became more familiar and developed through gigs **after** recording. [Dan Tepfer interview](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842) These statements establish ongoing reinterpretation as persistent, while any one instrumentation or intensity level is project-specific.

**Preset period decision:** reference the mature quartet and early independent-leader continuum, approximately **1960–72**, with *The Real McCoy* as the clearest bounded center. Do not advertise a whole-career simulation.

## 4. Persistent traits versus period/project traits

### Persistent or strongly recurring traits

- **An audible foundation plus freedom:** establish a tonal/root feeling, then allow open voicings and line movement, eventually resolving or returning. This is Tyner’s own explanation, not merely later theory shorthand. [Tepfer](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)
- **Rhythmic harmony:** chords function as attacks, pushes, and structural punctuation, not only as sustained vertical labels. The NEA characterizes Tyner’s quartet role as using richly textured harmonies as rhythmic devices. [NEA](https://www.arts.gov/honors/jazz/mccoy-tyner)
- **Contrast and pacing:** fiery passages coexist with ballads, blues, silence, thinner texture, and different ensemble formats.
- **Listening and response:** Tyner repeatedly defined good group playing through mutual listening rather than dominance. [Innerviews interview](https://www.innerviews.org/inner/mccoy-tyner)
- **Personal development rather than formula:** he resisted naming “his scales,” said he did not analyze himself technically, and described sound as an identity grown through doing. [Jazz Resource Center](http://www.jazzcenter.org/tyner/interview_2005.htm); [Tepfer](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)
- **Blues/song/formal grounding:** early standards and blues, later popular-song and big-band projects, and his description of melody developing into beginning/middle/return all contradict a purely static modal identity.
- **Acoustic-piano embodiment:** touch, pedaling, resonance, register, and dynamics are part of the result. Tyner explicitly tied his musical self to acoustic piano and avoided making fusion/electronics his core medium. [Innerviews](https://www.innerviews.org/inner/mccoy-tyner)

### Period- or project-specific traits

- The **Coltrane Quartet’s** repertoire, spiritual framing, long improvisational arcs, Garrison ostinati, and Jones’s polyrhythm belong to that ensemble.
- The especially concentrated modal/open-block language of the classic quartet and *The Real McCoy* should not be generalized to every standard, ballad, or later record.
- The larger horn palette of *Tender Moments*, harp and ensemble colors of *Extensions*, added percussion/cultural emphasis of *Asante* and *Sahara*, later big band, Latin band, strings, singer, and guitars are separate arrangements—not interchangeable “Tyner” parameters.
- Extreme loudness or density is event- and ensemble-dependent. Tyner specifically praised Jones for coming down dynamically and using brushes.
- A particular omitted pitch, hexatonic collection, bass move, chord plan, or famous vamp may be composition-specific. Tyner discussed pitch omission as a way to keep “Passion Dance” open, not as a universal law. [Tepfer](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)

## 5. Relationship to peers and tradition; attribution of roles

### Shared vocabulary

Fourth-built voicings, pentatonic collections, suspended sonorities, pedal points, vamps, blues language, and modal improvisation all have broader histories. Powell, Monk, Richie Powell, Red Garland, Debussy, Ravel, Stravinsky, Miles Davis’s groups, and Philadelphia peers form part of the relevant background. A product must not call stacked fourths “Tyner’s invention.” Satterthwaite and Iverson both warn, in different ways, against treating familiar pedagogical labels as the whole musician. [Satterthwaite](https://doi.org/10.12794/metadc1703303); [Iverson](https://ethaniverson.com/mccoy-tyners-revolution/)

### What is unusually characteristic in the scoped corpus

The characteristic contribution is the **integration** of strong, resonant open voicings; percussive but dynamically aware attacks; a firm left-hand/rhythmic foundation; mobile right-hand lines; blues and pentatonic resources; and large-scale tension/release inside a listening ensemble. The sound is recognizable because these act together, not because a chord stack alone bears an authorial fingerprint.

### Tyner versus Coltrane and the ensemble

- **Coltrane:** leader, principal composer/repertoire setter, saxophone soloist, and conceptual directional force. Tyner said the quartet needed Coltrane’s leadership even though all members shaped the music. [JazzWeekly](https://www.jazzweekly.com/interviews/tyner.htm)
- **Tyner:** an originating co-shaper of the quartet sound, not a passive executor. His voicings, rhythmic foundation, choices to push or withdraw, and compositions belong to him. Tyner recalled that Coltrane generally supplied sketches rather than dictated detail. [Jerry Jazz Musician](https://www.jerryjazzmusician.com/mccoy-tyner-talks-about-john-coltrane-and-the-recording-of-a-love-supreme/)
- **Elvin Jones:** a responsive source of pulse, cross-rhythm, dynamic contour, and phrase commentary—not merely “loud drums.”
- **Jimmy Garrison:** tonal/time anchor and interactive bassist, essential to the quartet’s freedom-with-return.
- **Leader dates:** on *The Real McCoy* Tyner controls composition and project frame, but Henderson, Carter, and Jones remain co-creators of the realized intensity.

The correct historical claim is **collective authorship under a leader**, with distinctive individual contributions. The preset may reference Tyner’s arrangement role; it must not market the full quartet sound as his isolated property.

## 6. Within-phrase, across-section, and across-project evolution

### Historically grounded temporal model

`Anchor/statement → punctuated support → mobile intensification → space/lay-out → audible return or resolution`

- **Anchor/statement:** establish root, mode, bass ostinato, theme, or familiar harmonic frame.
- **Punctuated support:** use discrete chordal attacks and repeated/varied shapes to support rather than fill every subdivision.
- **Mobile intensification:** increase attack rate, register span, voicing displacement, and rhythmic opposition as the soloist/ensemble creates room.
- **Space/lay-out:** thin to a held tone, sparse punctuation, or silence so the live voice can continue unbounded.
- **Return/resolution:** restore the anchor, theme, cadence, or recognizable pulse. The return need not be common-practice V–I, but it must sound intentional.

This model derives directly from Tyner’s statements about giving a soloist a push, laying out, maintaining a point of return, moving through open voicings, and resolving them. It is safer than reverse-engineering one transcription into universal rules.

### Across sections

A section should not merely transpose the same fourth-stack generator. Contrast may be achieved by:

- moving from vamp/modal openness to a blues or composed bridge;
- changing density and register while retaining tonal identity;
- allowing a soloist-led high point, then returning to the theme;
- changing ensemble role (chords to silence; accompaniment to a brief answering figure);
- reinterpreting recurrent material rather than replaying it identically.

*The Real McCoy* demonstrates project-level contrast among upheaval, contemplation, polyrhythm, and blues. Tyner’s account of *A Love Supreme* describes cumulative growth across years and within a suite-like session, while his later comments on rerecording “Passion Dance” show that the same composition changes with personnel and period. [Blue Note](https://www.bluenote.com/spotlight/the-real-mccoy/); [Tepfer](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)

### Across projects/career

`Bebop/blues/classical formation → quartet modal laboratory → independent post-bop consolidation → broader African-oriented and ensemble palette → later contrast among trio, big band, Latin, orchestral, popular-song, and guitar settings`

This is cumulative, not a replacement sequence. Tyner explicitly described development as building on prior periods rather than playing the same thing for ten or twenty years. [Tepfer](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)

## 7. Common caricatures and disallowed product claims

### Caricatures to avoid

1. **“McCoy Tyner = parallel fourths + minor pentatonic.”** This erases bebop, dominant/function-derived language, blues, standards, melody, form, touch, and interaction.
2. **“Modal = static.”** Tyner used openness to enable movement and meaningful resolution.
3. **“Forceful = always loud, dense, and sustaining.”** Jones used brushes; Tyner laid out; *The Real McCoy* contains ballads and relaxed blues; pedal was lifted to prevent blur.
4. **“Any fourth stack is authentic.”** Voicing, register, rhythm, anchor, resolution, and context decide whether a fourth-rich chord is functional.
5. **“The pianist controls every moment.”** Tyner sometimes removed harmonic boundaries and let the soloist lead.
6. **“The Coltrane Quartet sound is Tyner alone.”** Coltrane’s leadership/compositions, Jones’s rhythmic behavior, and Garrison’s foundation are irreducible.
7. **“African influence” as an exotic percussion switch.** Tyner connected it to sustained adolescent study, dance, cultural identification, and collaborators; generic “tribal” copy would be historically irresponsible.
8. **One continuous career style.** His media, ensembles, repertoire, and interpretation changed substantially.
9. **Spirituality as a sonic effect.** Tyner discussed spiritual commitment and collective respect; a scale or reverb setting cannot manufacture it.

### Disallowed product/UI/marketing claims

Do not say:

- “Play exactly like McCoy Tyner,” “authentic McCoy Tyner voicings,” or “Tyner in a box.”
- “Recreates the John Coltrane Quartet,” “the *A Love Supreme* sound,” or “the *Passion Dance* progression.”
- “McCoy Tyner invented quartal harmony/modal jazz.”
- “Uses Tyner’s signature scale,” especially since Tyner rejected that kind of self-labeling.
- “Works with any input” or “turns any melody into Tyner.” The input, space, tonal anchoring, and ensemble response matter.
- “African rhythm” unless a specific, accurately researched rhythmic practice is actually implemented; this report does not establish one.
- “Telepathic interplay,” “spiritual transformation,” or any claim that deterministic MIDI reproduces human communication, acoustic touch, cultural experience, or belief.
- “Official,” “endorsed,” or artist-approved.

Acceptable bounded copy: **“An original live-arrangement preset informed by open fourth-rich voicings, modal anchoring, rhythmic chordal punctuation, and responsive density documented in selected McCoy Tyner performances from the 1960s and early 1970s. It does not reproduce the artist or any recording.”**

## 8. Primary evidence and recording checkpoints

1. **Tyner on accompaniment (1963):** accompaniment can push or suggest, but laying out can free the soloist; the ensemble still shares a foundation and point of return. Scans/transcription are reproduced in Iverson’s essay. [Iverson](https://ethaniverson.com/mccoy-tyners-revolution/)
2. **Tyner on quartet authorship and development:** Coltrane led, all four shaped the music, and *A Love Supreme* represented a matured plateau rather than a standalone formula. [JazzWeekly](https://www.jazzweekly.com/interviews/tyner.htm); [Jerry Jazz Musician](https://www.jerryjazzmusician.com/mccoy-tyner-talks-about-john-coltrane-and-the-recording-of-a-love-supreme/)
3. **“Acknowledgement,” *A Love Supreme*:** around **0:51**, after bass and drums enter, the piano statement is a useful listening checkpoint for rotating fourth-rich sonorities over a stable ostinato; the broader performance demonstrates accumulation and collective response. This timestamp is a study locator, not permission to copy the passage. [Published analytical discussion](https://robewing.substack.com/p/mccoy-tyner4thshexatonicspentatonics)
4. ***The Real McCoy* (1967):** compare “Passion Dance” with “Search for Peace,” “Contemplation,” “Four by Five,” and “Blues on the Corner.” Their contrast is primary evidence against one fixed intensity/state. Blue Note documents the recording date, personnel, and distinct track-level characters. [Blue Note](https://www.bluenote.com/spotlight/the-real-mccoy/)
5. **Tyner on open voicing and resolution (2008):** establish a root, keep the sound open, move among related sonorities, and resolve convincingly; careful pedal lifting prevents one sonority from smearing into the next. [Tepfer](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)
6. **Tyner on modal labels (2005):** a vamp creates freedom, but he resisted reducing his music to technical names or a named scale. [Jazz Resource Center](http://www.jazzcenter.org/tyner/interview_2005.htm)
7. **Tyner on ensemble dynamics (2008):** Jones listened, complemented phrases, used brushes under piano solos, and reduced volume when necessary. [Tepfer](https://dantepfer.com/blog/?p=842)
8. **Oral-history baseline:** the Smithsonian’s 76-page NEA Jazz Master interview is the principal archival life-history check for training, chronology, Coltrane relationship, and later work. [Smithsonian transcript](https://americanhistory.si.edu/sites/default/files/file-uploader/Tyner-McCoy-Transcription-2020.pdf)

## 9. Concrete, testable implications for a live arrangement preset

These are arrangement requirements, not claims of exact stylistic emulation.

### Required invariants

1. **Anchor before expansion:** after transport start or a new section, the generated layer must expose a stable tonal/root relationship before introducing more remote displacement. **Test:** in the first 1–2 bars, at least one generated event contains or clearly supports the configured center; after an intensified passage, the layer returns to that relationship.
2. **Fourth-rich, not fourth-only:** open voicings may prominently contain perfect fourths/fifths, but the generator must allow other intervals and voice-leading. **Test:** a 16-bar output contains fourth-rich sonorities but does not reduce every event to an identical transposed stack.
3. **Punctuation and space:** accompaniment must include attacks separated by rests/holds and permit a lay-out state. **Test:** sustained monophonic input does not cause continuous retriggering; at least one phrase state reduces event density or becomes silent.
4. **Input-responsive intensity:** denser/faster player gestures may raise voicing activity; sparse gestures must not trigger an automatic note storm. **Test:** compare identical transport with sparse versus active input; output density changes in the same direction within capped limits.
5. **Resolution/return:** chromatic or displaced sonorities require a bounded return to center, theme-state, or consonant anchor. **Test:** after the highest-tension state, the next state resolves within a defined bar count rather than wandering indefinitely.
6. **Register separation:** generated harmony must leave the lead input intelligible. **Test:** avoid persistent unison/dense seconds around the player’s median pitch; move accompaniment below, above, or thin it.
7. **Dynamic arc, not permanent maximum:** force should emerge through increasing attack rate/register/density and then recede. **Test:** a 16–32-bar acceptance run contains at least three distinguishable density states and no maximum-density state lasting the whole form.
8. **Collective-role honesty:** the preset supplies one generated arrangement layer, not simulated Coltrane/Jones/Garrison parts. **Test:** UI and metadata use the approximation statement and do not name a copied track, solo, bass ostinato, or drum pattern.

### Minimal state machine

`ANCHOR → PUNCTUATE → INTENSIFY → LAY_OUT → RETURN`

- Trigger **PUNCTUATE** after a clear motif or repeated tonal center.
- Trigger **INTENSIFY** only after player activity or section progression crosses a threshold; cap polyphony and retrigger rate.
- Trigger **LAY_OUT** after a density peak, at a phrase boundary, or when the player sustains/opens space.
- Trigger **RETURN** at the next strong boundary; re-establish the center with fewer notes before restarting the cycle.

This is preferable to generating constant parallel planing. It operationalizes the documented push/withdraw/return behavior with the fewest historical assumptions.

### Abstract acceptance examples

- **Input A:** center D; one sustained scale-degree `1` for two bars, then a short `1–♭3–4–5` motif in bars 3–4. **Expected:** sparse open support/hold in bars 1–2; two or three rhythmically separated fourth-rich responses in bars 3–4; no continuous block stream; return to a D-centered sonority at the phrase boundary.
- **Input B:** center F; repeated offbeat `5` gestures for four bars, then one bar of silence. **Expected:** accompaniment progresses from sparse punctuation to a capped density peak, does not double every attack mechanically, and enters lay-out/thin state during the player’s silence before a restrained return.
- **Input C:** section change from F-centered vamp to B♭ blues-like frame. **Expected:** do not transpose an unchanged quartal pattern wholesale; reduce density at the boundary, recognize the new anchor, then rebuild activity.

### Explicit capability limits

A MIDI harmony preset cannot reproduce Tyner’s acoustic-piano touch, pedal half-lifting, voicing under the hand, microtiming, decades-long ensemble rapport, Coltrane’s leadership, Jones’s responsive dynamics, Garrison’s time, or the cultural/spiritual meanings Tyner attached to the work. If the engine lacks phrase/section memory, input-activity tracking, dynamic response, or a true lay-out state, the honest implementation is a simpler sparse-to-dense cycle with a fixed return—not marketing those missing behaviors as interaction.

## 10. Confidence and gaps

### High confidence

- The 1960–65 quartet plus 1967–72 leader period is a defensible scope for forceful open/modal voicing.
- Tyner combined fourth-rich sonorities with blues, bebop, standards, functional/dominant resources, melody, and form.
- Openness, resolution, rhythmic punctuation, dynamic contrast, laying out, and ensemble listening are more faithful invariants than constant parallel fourths.
- Coltrane led the quartet; Tyner was an independent co-shaper; Jones and Garrison were indispensable to the realized texture.
- Later career settings make any whole-career single-style claim untenable.

These claims rest on multiple direct interviews, the Smithsonian archive, institutional biography, label documentation, discography, and scholarship.

### Medium confidence

- The exact boundary “1960–72” is a product-scoping judgment, not a period label Tyner used.
- The proposed five-state arrangement model is a defensible abstraction of documented behavior, not a transcription-derived universal form.
- *The Real McCoy* is the best center for this preset; another researcher could reasonably center mature quartet performances instead.

### Low confidence / unresolved gaps

- No systematic measure-by-measure comparison across the full corpus was completed here; exact voicing distributions, register statistics, onset densities, and section-transition frequencies remain unquantified.
- The report does not establish one precise African rhythmic model and therefore licenses none.
- Interview recollections given decades later may compress chronology or causality.
- The “Acknowledgement” timestamp relies on a published analytical locator; recording editions can shift by seconds.
- This report did not verify what HarmonyEngine/Lane currently supports. Implementation should audit phrase memory, density tracking, transport boundaries, velocity mapping, register avoidance, and lay-out behavior before promising the state model.

**Next evidence step if greater fidelity is required:** license/access scores or create lawful analytical transcriptions of short, non-distributed excerpts from at least one performance in each scoped stratum (*My Favorite Things*, *A Love Supreme*, *The Real McCoy*, *Sahara*), then measure voicing interval content, attack density, silence, register, and return timing. Do not expand scope to the full career unless later ensemble types are separately modeled.

- [Jerry Jazz Musician, *A Love Supreme* interview](https://www.jerryjazzmusician.com/mccoy-tyner-talks-about-john-coltrane-and-the-recording-of-a-love-supreme/) — direct 2001 interview on quartet leadership, form, communication, and the album as culmination.

### Dropped or not relied upon

- **Wikipedia biographies/discographies** — useful navigation, but official discography, NEA, archives, and label sources were available.
- **AllMusic biography/reviews** — broad secondary summaries duplicated stronger institutional and primary evidence.
- **Discogs and MusicBrainz** — release metadata was redundant with official/label discography.
- **Commercial lesson sites and downloadable “Tyner voicing” PDFs** — pedagogical simplification, uncertain provenance, and high risk of reinforcing the exact caricature under review.
- **Fan album blogs and anonymous listening notes** — potentially useful leads, but weaker than recordings, interviews, scholarship, and label archives.
- **Unsourced claims that Tyner single-handedly invented quartal harmony or left Coltrane for one simple reason** — excluded as reductive and historically under-supported.
- **Generic “African jazz” explainers** — excluded because they do not document Tyner’s specific study, collaborators, rhythms, or projects.
