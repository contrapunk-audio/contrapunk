# Research: Preset 08 — Suspension Garland (Role A: history and primary sources)

**Retrieval date:** 2026-07-15 (UTC)  
**Preset claim under review:** “prepared suspensions resolve across strong beats”; Fux/Palestrina reference; current Species IV baseline; transport required.

## Summary

The coherent intersection is narrow but real: Fux’s fourth species isolates a repeatable pedagogical chain—consonant preparation on the metrically weaker half, the same pitch sustained into a stronger position where another voice makes it dissonant, then downward stepwise consonant resolution—while Palestrina’s four-voice practice frequently uses accented, longer-note suspensions inside a much freer mixture of imitation, passing motion, changing voice counts, and cadential pacing. “Across strong beats” should therefore mean **a note begins consonantly before a metric accent, is retained across that accent as a controlled dissonance, and resolves after it (normally down by step)**, not “any long note crossing a barline,” not “all voices suspend,” and not “Palestrina wrote continuous fourth-species exercises.” [Fux/Mann, pp. 55–63](https://ia904500.us.archive.org/23/items/pdfy-_OKTX_2lKsXA_zvN/Counterpoint,%20Fux,%20by%20Alfred%20Mann_text.pdf) [Anders et al., paras. 17–22, Table 1](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-244/)

## 1. Scope and corpus

1. **Fux:** Johann Joseph Fux, *Gradus ad Parnassum* (Vienna, 1725), Part II dialogue between “Aloysius” (the idealized Palestrina teacher) and “Josephus.” The operative primary-source section is two-part fourth species, Mann translation pp. 55–63; the later multi-part “ligature” discussion begins p. 94. This is a graded exercise system, not a transcription of sixteenth-century composing practice. The 1725 Latin facsimile confirms title, date, and publication context. [1725 facsimile](http://tmg.huma-num.fr/xtf/view?docId=bookreader%2FFux_1725%2FFux_1725.xml) [Mann translation](https://ia904500.us.archive.org/23/items/pdfy-_OKTX_2lKsXA_zvN/Counterpoint,%20Fux,%20by%20Alfred%20Mann_text.pdf)
2. **Palestrina, selected score witnesses:** the four-voice motets *Sicut cervus* (first published 1587, *Motectorum liber secundus*, no. 10) and *Super flumina Babylonis* (CPDL catalogs four voices and first publication in the same 1587 book, no. 3). Modern CPDL transcriptions preserve four independent parts but may modernize clefs, barlines, pitch, or note values; claims tied to modern measure numbers are edition-specific. [*Sicut cervus* catalog/score witnesses](https://www.cpdl.org/wiki/index.php/Sicut_cervus_%28Giovanni_Pierluigi_da_Palestrina%29) [*Super flumina* catalog/score witnesses](http://cpdl.org/wiki/index.php/Super_flumina_Babylonis_a_4_(Giovanni_Pierluigi_da_Palestrina))
3. **Palestrina, corpus check:** Hanson's enumeration covers all 210 four-voice Ordinary movements in forty four-voice masses (18,313 modern-edition bars), using the Fratelli Scalera complete edition. Anders et al. use the first 36 Agnus movements in music21’s Palestrina corpus; that sample is explicitly arbitrary and all happen to be encoded in 4/2. These corpora are used to test whether “garlands” are representative, not to collapse all Palestrina into two motets. [Hanson](https://symposium.music.org/volume-23/articles-1752877059/enumeration-of-dissonance-in-the-masses-of-palestrina) [Anders et al., paras. 24–39](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-244/)
4. **Excluded scope:** no claim to copy a Palestrina melody; no claim that two late motets represent every mass, motet, madrigal, voice count, or career phase; no claim that Fux’s eighteenth-century rules are a documentary account of Palestrina’s workshop.

## 2. Historical and aesthetic context

- **Palestrina’s setting.** Palestrina (c. 1525–1594) worked chiefly in Roman sacred institutions: organist/singer at Palestrina, Julian Chapel director from 1551, then St John Lateran and Santa Maria Maggiore, returning to St Peter’s in 1571. His output spans over 105 masses and 250 motets and uses cantus-firmus, parody, canon, free composition, chant paraphrase, and differing voice counts. Suspension treatment belongs to a broad vocal contrapuntal language, not a standalone personal trademark. [Britannica, “Life” and “Music”](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Giovanni-Pierluigi-da-Palestrina)
- **Counter-Reformation context, without the myth.** Concerns about sacred decorum and intelligible words formed part of Catholic reform, but the Council of Trent’s final action on music was limited and delegated details locally. The story that *Missa Papae Marcelli* single-handedly “saved polyphony” is a later legend, not a sound basis for the preset. [Monson, “The Council of Trent Revisited,” abstract](https://doi.org/10.1525/jams.2002.55.1.1)
- **Fux’s setting.** Fux (c. 1660–1741) was an Austrian Baroque court and church musician; *Gradus* appeared late in his career in 1725. It preserves and systematizes *stile antico* for training through deliberately abstract cantus-firmus tasks. The dialogue’s Palestrina persona signals aspiration and authority, not identity between the exercise and historical repertoire. [FUX online](https://fux-online.at/cms_seite.php?content=1&lang=E&menu=1) [Dodi, “preservation of stile antico”](https://periodicos.ufrn.br/artresearchjournal/article/download/31434/16949/110579)

## 3. Career/style evolution (only where defensible)

- **Palestrina:** early Roman advancement and first publications (mass book, 1554; madrigals, 1555) precede several institutional moves; from his 1571 return to St Peter’s through the 1580s–90s he published heavily across masses, motets, and madrigals. The selected 1587 motets are therefore mature/late-career sacred works, but their four-voice texture is only one part of a varied output. [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Giovanni-Pierluigi-da-Palestrina)
- **No defensible suspension “career arc.”** Hanson specifically looked for chronological patterns across the mass corpus and found none for the nine enumerated dissonance devices except a marked decline in *nota cambiata* usage. It would be unsupported to advertise mature Palestrina as “more suspension-heavy” or to turn this preset into a career-evolution narrative. [Hanson, discussion following Table 1](https://symposium.music.org/volume-23/articles-1752877059/enumeration-of-dissonance-in-the-masses-of-palestrina)
- **Fux:** the relevant evolution is reception and abstraction: a late-career 1725 pedagogy became enormously influential, while Fux’s own stylistically varied compositional career is outside this preset. Do not label the generated result “Fux’s compositional style.”

## 4. Persistent traits versus work-/system-specific traits

### Persistent or corpus-supported

- Suspensions are accented dissonances: in Anders et al.’s sample they cluster on strong beat 1 and strong beat 3, are approached by pitch repetition (a held/tied tone), and resolve by downward step; their dissonant portions are quarter or half notes in the encoding. The study found 265 beat-1 and 313 beat-3 cases, versus larger passing-tone categories on weak beats. [Anders et al., Table 1 and paras. 40–52](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-244/)
- In Hanson’s much larger four-voice mass corpus, 4–3 was the most frequent of the nine enumerated devices, averaging about once every four bars; 7–6 averaged about once every ten bars. The corpus also includes 9–8, 2–3, 2–1, double suspensions, and contextual exceptions. [Hanson, Table 1 and accompanying text](https://symposium.music.org/volume-23/articles-1752877059/enumeration-of-dissonance-in-the-masses-of-palestrina)
- The texture remains contrapuntal and mixed: weak-beat passing/neighbor motion coexists with stronger-beat suspensions. PeerJ’s notated primary example, *Missa De Beata Marie Virginis* II, Agnus, mm. 24–26, places passing tones, a neighbor tone, and a suspension in close proximity. [Anders et al., Fig. 1](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-244/#fig-1)

### Fux-/exercise-specific or selected-work-specific

- Continuous half-note-against-whole-note syncopation, a fixed cantus firmus, and maximizing a chain of ligatures are fourth-species constraints. They are useful for making the audible mechanism reliable, but they are not a statistical portrait of complete Palestrina movements.
- The two selected 1587 motets’ text-responsive imitation and phrase design should not be reduced to a suspension generator. Their edition histories also show transposition, changed note values, and added barlines in some modern scores. [CPDL *Sicut cervus* edition notes](https://www.cpdl.org/wiki/index.php/Sicut_cervus_%28Giovanni_Pierluigi_da_Palestrina%29)
- Cadential suspension density and exact interval species depend on bass/voice context. “Always 4–3” would exaggerate the most common type into the only type.

## 5. Relationship to peers and tradition

- Prepared dissonance, smooth stepwise vocal motion, imitation, modal organization, and cadential formulas were shared sixteenth-century contrapuntal vocabulary, not Palestrina’s private invention. Palestrina’s masses frequently reuse music by predecessors/contemporaries through parody technique; his motets and masses also draw on chant. [Britannica, “Music”](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Giovanni-Pierluigi-da-Palestrina/Music)
- Lassus and Victoria belong to the same broad late-sixteenth-century sacred-polyphonic field, with locally and personally different practices. Hanson’s Palestrina-only counts justify a bounded Palestrina corpus claim, not uniqueness versus peers.
- Fux belongs to a later *stile antico* reception: he turns inherited polyphonic behavior into sequential species. The coherent reference is therefore **Palestrina corpus practice filtered through Fuxian pedagogy**, not a generic average of two coeval composers.

## 6. What “prepared suspensions across strong beats” honestly means

A qualifying event needs all four conditions:

1. **Preparation:** a voice attacks or is already sounding a pitch while that pitch forms a consonance with the controlling contrapuntal context.
2. **Retention across an accent:** the same pitch continues (tie/rearticulation equivalence in data may be necessary) while another voice changes, making the retained pitch dissonant on a metrically stronger position.
3. **Resolution:** the suspended voice then moves, normally **down by diatonic step**, into consonance.
4. **Metric hierarchy:** “strong” is relative. In a modern 4/2 representation, beat 1 is strongest and beat 3 is also strong; a suspension need not cross a modern barline. Renaissance source notation did not encode modern barlines, so transport should model pulse/accent, not fetishize UI barlines. [Anders et al., paras. 40–46 and Table 1](https://peerj.com/articles/cs-244/)

Thus the catalog wording is acceptable if read as **prepared notes retained into accents and resolved afterward**. It is misleading if read as “resolution occurs on the strong beat”: normally the *dissonant suspension* occupies the accent and resolution follows. It is also misleading if any held common tone is counted without testing preparation, dissonance, and resolution.

## 7. Documented intersection and excluded differences

### Intersection retained by the preset

- consonant preparation;
- sustained/repeated pitch into a metrically accented dissonance;
- controlled stepwise resolution, predominantly downward;
- chaining often enough to make syncopated tension/release audible;
- independent four-voice context rather than block-chord planing.

### Differences deliberately excluded

- **From Fux:** fixed two-note-against-one exercise rhythm, immutable cantus firmus, and near-continuous ligature chains are pedagogical restrictions, not requirements for historical Palestrina texture.
- **From Palestrina:** text setting, modal/ficta decisions, imitative points, cadential planning, variable rhythmic surface, voice-entry/withdrawal, sacred genre, and actual melodic material are not reproduced by “Species IV baseline.”
- **Historical gap:** Fux wrote roughly 130 years after Palestrina’s death. His Aloysius is an idealized authority; the preset must not claim direct reconstruction.

## 8. Common caricatures and myths to avoid

1. **“Palestrina saved polyphony at Trent.”** Unsupported legend; Trent did not simply ban polyphony and reverse itself after one mass. [Monson](https://doi.org/10.1525/jams.2002.55.1.1)
2. **“Fux equals Palestrina.”** Fux supplies a durable abstraction of *stile antico*, not a neutral empirical transcription.
3. **“Fourth species means every downbeat is dissonant.”** Consonant ligatures, breaks in species, cadential adjustments, and free counterpoint exist.
4. **“Suspension means any overlap.”** Without consonant preparation, accented dissonance, and controlled resolution, it is merely a held tone or another nonharmonic event.
5. **“Palestrina is uniformly smooth/pure.”** Corpus studies show varied dissonance categories, double suspensions, rare exceptions, and differing distributions; academic “purity” can rigidify a flexible style. [Hanson](https://symposium.music.org/volume-23/articles-1752877059/enumeration-of-dissonance-in-the-masses-of-palestrina) [Britannica, “Music”](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Giovanni-Pierluigi-da-Palestrina/Music)
6. **“A garland is constant suspension saturation.”** Hanson’s averages support recurrent 4–3 and 7–6 motion, not one suspension in every voice on every accent.

## 10. Confidence, disagreements, and gaps

### Confidence by claim

- **High:** Fux fourth species codifies consonant preparation → accented retained dissonance → downward-step resolution; pp. 55–63.
- **High:** suspensions are frequent but mixed with other dissonance types in Palestrina; supported independently by a very large enumerated mass corpus and a smaller computational Agnus sample.
- **High:** 4–3 is substantially more common than 7–6 in Hanson’s four-voice corpus; “all types equal” would be false.
- **High:** the Trent “savior” story is a myth/overstatement.
- **Medium:** the two 1587 motets are suitable mature four-voice exemplars for source consultation; exact suspension locations were not asserted because accessible transcriptions differ in bars/note values and this run did not complete a note-by-note diplomatic comparison.
- **Medium:** a transport-aware Species IV generator can honestly evoke the intersection if UI copy says “inspired by prepared suspension practice,” not “authentic Palestrina.”

### Disagreements/qualification

- Hanson shows why textbook generalizations can fail: one cited manual calls 7–6 and 4–3 simply the most common suspended dissonances, but the corpus shows far more 4–3s; another calls 7–6/4–3 double suspension rare, while Hanson counts 111 in the four-voice masses. Corpus evidence should override tidy parity claims.
- CPDL’s *Super flumina* page gives “first published 1587,” while an edition note says its transcription derives through an 1875 edition from Gardano’s 1564 book of festal motets. Publication history therefore remains unresolved here; it does not affect the suspension claim but should be checked against RISM/original partbooks before final catalog metadata.
- Anders et al.’s algorithm intentionally misses complex cases and uses modern accent weights plus an arbitrary 36-Agnus sample. Its beat-1/beat-3 clusters corroborate, but do not replace, score reading or Hanson’s larger hand-enumerated corpus.

### Gaps / blocked or deferred work

- The Cambridge chapter on Fux’s theoretical precursors/successors was abstract-only/paywalled; skipped rather than paraphrased beyond available evidence.
- The Internet Archive restricted Jeppesen volume was not used as direct evidence; secondary summaries of Jeppesen were not promoted to primary evidence.
- Exact suspension-by-suspension measure lists for *Sicut cervus* and *Super flumina* require choosing one critical/diplomatic edition, then checking all four parts against the early print; modern CPDL measure numbers are not safely interchangeable.
- Hanson’s tables are image-based; the prose supplies key ratios and totals, but a reproducible dataset was not available in the article.
- No reliable evidence found for a chronological evolution of Palestrina’s suspension practice. Treat that as unknown, not absent.

## Preset-facing historical judgment

**Historically defensible bounded claim:** “A transport-synchronized fourth-species study inspired by Fux’s pedagogy and recurrent suspension practice in Palestrina’s four-voice sacred counterpoint: prepared held tones become accented dissonances and normally resolve downward by step.”
