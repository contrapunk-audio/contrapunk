# Role A history / primary-source report — preset 04 “Mensuration Web”

**Named composer:** Johannes Ockeghem (in modern scholarship also **Jean/Johannes de Okeghem**; see naming note below)  
**Central work:** *Missa prolationum* / *Missa prolacionum*  
**Scope:** historical and notational basis for an abstract preset description; no melodic material reproduced  
**Retrieval date for web sources:** 2026-07-02

## Executive judgment

“Mensuration Web” has a strong historical anchor if it evokes Ockeghem’s *Missa prolationum* as a network of paired canons whose shared written material is interpreted under different mensurations. The safe short description is: **“paired canons derive voices from shared written lines under different mensural rules, creating coordinated unequal rhythmic spans.”**

The phrase **“one motif at simultaneous proportional speeds” is defensible only as a loose explanation of one mensuration-canon pair**: the same notated succession can yield temporally unequal realizations when read under different mensuration/prolation signs. It is **not** an exact account of the whole four-voice Mass. The work is normally a *double* canon—two source lines generating two paired realizations—not one motif copied into all four voices at four freely chosen playback rates. “Speed” here is a resultant relation among written values under mensural division, not a modern BPM automation lane, and the signs do not by themselves prescribe one historically certain performed tempo.

## 1. Chronology and context

### Career frame

Ockeghem/Okeghem belongs to the central decades of the fifteenth century, between the older generation of Guillaume Du Fay and Gilles Binchois and the later generation represented by Josquin. Recent documentary synthesis places his birth probably in the early-to-mid 1420s at Saint-Ghislain, while older reference works often give a wider “ca. 1410/1430” range. His first secure employment is as a paid adult singer at Notre-Dame, Antwerp, in 1443–44. A fragmentary account places him in Charles I, duke of Bourbon’s chapel around 1447/48. He entered French royal service by the early 1450s, was directing the royal chapel by 1454 at the latest, and remained attached to the royal establishment through the reigns of Charles VII, Louis XI, and Charles VIII until his death in 1497. He also became canon and then treasurer of Saint-Martin of Tours. These were institutional, liturgical, administrative, and courtly roles—not the biography of an isolated puzzle-maker. [Ricercar Data Lab biography and archival references](https://ricercardatalab.cesr.univ-tours.fr/en/people/396/) **[high confidence]**

A useful primary-document anchor is the record of a solemn *Te Deum* sung by Ockeghem and his colleagues on 5 November 1458, cited as Paris, BnF, français 32511, fol. 210v. The same documentary synthesis cites his two rediscovered autograph signatures in Paris, BnF, français 2904, fols. 28 and 34. These records establish a working chapelmaster/singer and administrator; they do not date *Missa prolationum*. [Ricercar Data Lab](https://ricercardatalab.cesr.univ-tours.fr/en/people/396/) **[high confidence for the archival claims; medium until originals are independently collated]**

### Date of *Missa prolationum*

The Mass’s exact composition date and occasion are unknown. “Second half of the fifteenth century” is safe; narrower dates based chiefly on style remain hypotheses. The principal Chigi copy was produced only around 1498–1503, just after Ockeghem’s death, and therefore proves circulation by that point, not a date of composition. [DIAMM, Chigi Codex](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/943/) **[high confidence]**

Do not present the Mass as a securely “late” summation or as a youthful experiment. Without external dating evidence, either label overstates the chronology. **[high confidence that the date is unresolved]**

## 2. Terms that must not be collapsed

1. **Chronology** concerns when the composer lived, held posts, and wrote or transmitted a work. It cannot be inferred automatically from a manuscript’s copying date.
2. **Mensuration** is the notational system governing the hierarchical division of note values. In simplified terms, it includes relationships such as long-to-breve (*modus*), breve-to-semibreve (*tempus*), and semibreve-to-minim (*prolatio*). Mensuration signs are not equivalent to modern time signatures.
3. **Prolation** is specifically the division of the semibreve into minims—binary/minor or ternary/major—not a synonym for every kind of rhythmic proportion. The title *prolationum* foregrounds this domain, while the work’s technique is commonly called mensuration or prolation canon.
4. **Notation** is the physical written encoding: note shapes, mensuration signs, rests, coloration or other conventions, textual placement, and the manuscript layout through which singers derive realizations.
5. **Canon** is the rule-governed derivation of one voice from another. A mensuration canon applies different mensural readings to shared written material; pitch interval and temporal entry relation are additional variables and can change by section.
6. **Performed tempo** is the sounding pace selected by performers, historically related to tactus, note values, text, mensuration, and context. It is not uniquely recoverable as a modern metronome number from a mensuration sign alone.

The core preset error to avoid is translating “different mensurations” directly into “independent BPMs.” The voices are coordinated by one contrapuntal design; their unequal durations arise from rule-bound readings within that design.

## 3. Primary manuscript and score evidence

### A. Vatican City, Biblioteca Apostolica Vaticana, MS Chigi C.VIII.234

DIAMM describes the Chigi Codex as a parchment choirbook in black void mensural notation, its main corpus copied in Brussels/Mechelen around 1498–1503. It provides a stable Vatican facsimile link and IIIF manifest. The manuscript’s main corpus contains extensive Ockeghem repertory and attributes the opening of *Missa prolationum* to OCKEGHEM. [DIAMM source record](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/943/); [Vatican digital facsimile](https://digi.vatlib.it/view/MSS_Chig.C.VIII.234) **[high confidence]**

Stable movement-level locations in the modern foliation:

- Kyrie: fols. **106v–107**, with attribution “OCKEGHEM.” [DIAMM](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/compositions/21659/)
- Gloria: fols. **107v–109**. [DIAMM](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/compositions/99322/)
- Credo: fols. **109v–111**. [DIAMM](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/compositions/99323/)
- Sanctus/Benedictus: fols. **111v–113**. [DIAMM](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/compositions/99324/)
- Agnus Dei: fols. **113v–114**. [DIAMM](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/compositions/99325/)

The codex is especially important because its compressed canonic notation makes the generative rule visible: it presents mensuration canons rather than simply four normalized modern parts. That is primary evidence for “derivation under mensural rules,” but the physical copy postdates the composer and cannot tell us his exact original notation or intended metronomic tempo. **[high confidence]**

### B. Vienna, Österreichische Nationalbibliothek, Cod. 11883 Han

The other complete manuscript witness is Vienna, ÖNB Cod. 11883 Han, fols. **208–221**. DIAMM dates the composite manuscript to the first third of the sixteenth century, probably copied in the Low Countries, and explicitly notes that in this copy “all the mensural canons are transformed into uniform mensurations.” It links the ÖNB digitization. [DIAMM source record](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/1985/) **[high confidence]**

This is decisive evidence against treating the Chigi riddle-like appearance as the only historical identity of the music. A historical scribe transmitted the same Mass in resolved, uniform mensurations. William Watson’s 2020 article argues that these *resolutiones* deserve analytical and performative weight rather than dismissal as a merely simplified derivative. [Watson, “The Other Missa Prolationum,” *Journal of Musicology* 37/3 (2020), pp. 267–304, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1525/jm.2020.37.3.267) **[high confidence for the source contrast; medium for detailed arguments because full text was inaccessible in this run]**

### C. A fragmentary concordance

DIAMM also records Kyrie I in Stuttgart, Württembergische Landesbibliothek, D-Sl HB XVII 26, fols. **55v–56v**. This supports wider transmission of at least part of the Mass but does not replace the two complete witnesses. [DIAMM Kyrie record](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/compositions/21659/) **[high confidence]**

## 4. What the musical claim can honestly mean

### Defensible meaning

Within one paired mensuration canon, singers may read the same written sequence under distinct mensuration/prolation conditions. The written note succession is therefore shared while its sounding durations are not identical. Relative spans such as 2-against-3 can result at the relevant mensural level. In a product description, “one motif at proportional speeds” can serve as an introductory metaphor for this temporal stretching, provided “motif” means a shared notated line and “speed” means a relative rhythmic realization—not a loop replayed by a sampler. **[high confidence]**

### What it cannot mean

- **Not one four-way motif:** *Missa prolationum* characteristically uses two simultaneous two-voice canons (four voices derived from two written parts). Saying all four voices are copies of one motif is materially misleading.
- **Not four arbitrary rate knobs:** the relations are constrained by mensural syntax, tactus, entry disposition, interval of imitation, cadential coordination, and singable counterpoint.
- **Not necessarily simultaneous note-on:** “simultaneous” describes the coexistence of the canonic processes. Individual derived voices can be separated by rests/entry offsets; the work’s sections do not all reduce to four voices launching the same first event together.
- **Not one mechanism unchanged throughout:** canonic intervals and entry dispositions vary by section. The cycle’s unity is the persistent canonic/mensural principle, not one invariant transform.
- **Not performed tempo:** a relative 2:3 realization does not establish the absolute tactus or BPM. Different historically informed performances can choose different absolute paces while preserving the relation.
- **Not proof of audible arithmetic as the sole aesthetic:** the mensural construction may be analytically striking while the sounding result remains texted, cadential, vocal polyphony rather than an audible mathematical demonstration at every instant.

**Recommended one-line preset copy:** “Two written lines unfold as paired canons under contrasting mensural divisions, weaving coordinated unequal spans.”

**If the catalog requires the existing phrase:** qualify it as “shared motifs realized at simultaneous proportional rates,” pluralizing *motifs* and replacing “speeds” with “rates.”

## 5. Persistent style versus work-specific design

### Traits that can responsibly connect the preset to Ockeghem more broadly

- Sustained four-voice contrapuntal continuity and long-range coordination.
- Flexible treatment of voice ranges and a comparatively full lower register, consistent with Ockeghem’s documented stature as a singer and mass composer, though “dark bass sound” should not become a universal rule.
- Structural variety across Mass cycles rather than reliance on one standard procedure: surviving masses include works built from chant or song models, freely composed designs, and conspicuous rule-based constructions.
- Canonic thinking beyond this Mass is relevant: *Missa cuiusvis toni* and the chanson *Prenez sur moy* belong to the broader evidence for his interest in constrained or multiply realizable counterpoint. These works use different problems and should not be described as if they duplicate *Missa prolationum*.

These are broad tendencies, not a statistical fingerprint. **[medium confidence]**

### Work-specific traits

- The systematic, cycle-wide use of mensuration canons is the defining feature of *Missa prolationum*, not a default Ockeghem setting for every genre.
- Its double-canonic texture and progressive variation of canonic interval/disposition belong to this particular compositional project.
- The Chigi codex’s compressed visual presentation is source-specific. The Vienna witness proves that historical transmission could instead foreground resolved performance parts.

**[high confidence]**

### Career/style evolution

A neat early/middle/late stylistic ladder is not currently supportable because many works lack secure dates. Career documents show institutional continuity and widening prestige, but they do not map surviving Masses to exact decades. The honest account is one of **coexisting strategies**—cantus-firmus/parody-like use of pre-existing material, freer cyclic procedures, and exceptional technical designs—rather than a proven evolution from “simple” to “complex.” **[high confidence for the caution; medium for the broad stylistic grouping]**

## 6. Peers, shared vocabulary, and characteristic choices

Mensural ingenuity, canon, contrafact or borrowed material, Mass cycles, four-voice scoring, and *L’homme armé* treatment were shared fifteenth-century Franco-Flemish/Burgundian vocabulary. Du Fay, Binchois, Busnoys, Petrus de Domarto, and later Obrecht and Josquin provide the surrounding field; Ockeghem did not invent rhythmic proportion or canon.

What is characteristic in *Missa prolationum* is **scale and consistency of application**: double mensuration canon is made the organizing premise across the Ordinary rather than an isolated tour de force. Britannica’s concise description correctly identifies two two-part canons proceeding simultaneously under different mensurations, but “different rates of speed” should be read as a pedagogical gloss, not a complete theory of performance. [Britannica work entry](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Missa-prolationum) **[high confidence for the double-canon point; medium for comparative uniqueness]**

Direct teacher-pupil stories require caution. Ockeghem met or worked in the orbit of major contemporaries, and later composers honored him, but resemblance or praise does not prove a formal pedagogical lineage. **[medium-high confidence]**

## 7. Myths and caricatures to exclude

1. **“Ockeghem the mathematical eccentric.”** The technical Mass is real; reducing a 46-year royal and ecclesiastical career to puzzles is not. Documentary evidence shows sustained leadership, singing, administration, patronage, and liturgical work.
2. **“The entire Mass is one tune at four speeds.”** False as a literal description; it is generally two concurrent canonic pairs and sectional dispositions vary.
3. **“Mensuration signs are Renaissance BPM/time signatures.”** False. They govern value relationships and interact with tactus and convention; they do not uniquely encode modern bars or metronome tempo.
4. **“The Chigi notation is the original/autograph.”** Unsupported. Chigi is a posthumous copy, albeit a crucial and lavish source.
5. **“The resolved Vienna copy is an error or dumbing-down.”** Too strong. It is a historical witness with uniform mensurations and is central to current debate about how the work was conceptualized and performed.
6. **“Ockeghem lived nearly 100 years.”** A poetic lament wished, in effect, that he had reached a hundred; recent documentary synthesis does not take it as proof of a ca. 1410 birth.
7. **“Ockeghem” is the uncontested historical spelling.** Modern products can retain the familiar form, but two rediscovered signatures and early documents support “Okeghem.” A naming note is preferable to silently declaring one form an error.

## 8. Product-facing implications for “Mensuration Web”

- Keep the preset abstract: paired streams, unequal but locked subdivisions, staggered or converging entries, and cadential reconvergence are historically suggestive.
- Do not claim reconstruction of a particular Mass section unless the engine actually models its mensural relationships, pitch interval of imitation, rests, and cadence plan.
- A fixed global tempo with relative per-voice rhythmic transformations is closer to the historical metaphor than four unrelated tempos.
- Preserve voice independence and phrase-level alignment; merely time-stretching identical MIDI clips misses the two-source-line/double-canon design.
- “Web” is a modern metaphor, not a historical term. It is apt for interdependence but should not imply algorithmic networks or generative randomness in Ockeghem’s notation.
- No melodic quotation is needed or recommended; the preset can evoke the procedure without copying protected editions or presenting a false reconstruction.

## 11. Gaps and next steps

- The accepted preset catalog and Role A template were inaccessible in this revived run, so exact catalog wording and template field parity could not be checked. This report is standalone and scoped only to preset 04.
- The Chigi folios were catalog-verified, but every mensuration sign, rest, and section boundary was not independently transcribed from the images. A specialist source collation should precede any claim to model a named section exactly.
- Watson’s full article was blocked; only its abstract-level thesis and bibliographic metadata were used. Review pp. 267–304 for its statistical argument, readings of the Vienna *resolutiones*, and any tempo consequences.
- No autograph compositional source survives in the evidence reviewed. The relation of either complete witness to Ockeghem’s lost exemplar remains uncertain.
- Absolute historical tempo is intentionally unresolved. A performance-practice review using Ruth DeFord’s *Tactus, Mensuration, and Rhythm in Renaissance Music* and relevant fifteenth-century theorists would be required before specifying tactus or BPM.
- Claims about Ockeghem’s total surviving output, exact developmental chronology, and direct influence on named pupils were omitted where the available evidence did not support precision.
