# Phase 10.2 — Preset 12 “Planed Cathedral”
## Role A: independent history and primary-source report (Claude Debussy)

**Research role:** artist/style historian and primary-source researcher

**Retrieval date for all web sources:** 8 March 2025

**Preset claim under review:** “whole-tone or pentatonic parallel chord planes,” for a slow, spacious monophonic source line.

**Bottom line:** This can be an honest, bounded reference to Debussy’s mature piano language c. 1903–10 only if “planing” is treated as one color-forming process among pedals, modal/diatonic regions, register, resonance, dynamic terraces, and formal transformation. It must not implement “Debussy = whole-tone chords.”

## 1. Scope and corpus

### Principal, score-checked corpus

1. **_Estampes_: “Pagodes” (1903), especially mm. 1–14, 23–31, 37–45, 80–98.** The Durand score supplies the primary evidence: low resonant fifth/pedal, pentatonic layers differentiated by register and speed, parallel fourth/fifth writing at mm. 27ff., dynamic contrasts, and a very quiet coda. [Debussy, _Estampes_, Durand score (1903)](https://ks15.imslp.org/files/imglnks/usimg/c/cd/IMSLP594739-PMLP2408-Estampes.pdf) The Philharmonie de Paris dates composition to 1903 and the première by Ricardo Viñes to 9 January 1904. [Philharmonie de Paris, work record](https://edutheque.philharmoniedeparis.fr/0769180-estampes-de-claude-debussy.aspx?_lg=fr-FR)
2. **_Images_, Book I: “Reflets dans l’eau” (1905), especially mm. 1–8, 31–47, 68–74.** Used to test whether moving chord shapes are isolated effects or participate in continuous voice leading and section overlap. Keith Waters’s score-based account identifies a tonic pedal and displaced three-note chords in mm. 1–8; retained figuration over a changed bass at mm. 42–47; and planed G-flat/F/E-flat-minor triads bridging mm. 69–71 into the return. [Waters, “Other Good Bridges,” _Music Theory Online_ 18/3 (2012)](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.waters.html)
3. **_Préludes_, Book I: “Voiles” and “La cathédrale engloutie” (1910).** The first Durand edition is the primary score. [Debussy, _Préludes, premier livre_, Durand (1910)](https://vmirror.imslp.org/files/imglnks/usimg/0/0c/IMSLP521885-PMLP2394-Debussy_Claude-Pr%C3%A9ludes_1er_Livre_Durand_7687_scan.pdf) “Voiles” is the deliberately restricted whole-tone/pentatonic experiment; “La cathédrale” is the preset’s more complete model because its open fifths, pentatonic/diatonic regions, chordal blocks, register, dynamics, and returns form an evolving dramatic arc. In “La cathédrale,” relevant spans are mm. 1–5 (soft, widely separated open sonorities), 7–13 (moving chordal material over a pedal), 16–19 (B-pentatonic region linked to an E-flat six-note collection), 28–41 (massive chordal/“organ” disclosure), 42–46 (withdrawal), 47ff. (contrasting continuation), 72–83 (transformed return), and 84–89 (remote coda/after-image). Waters independently verifies the B-pentatonic-to-E-flat shift and motivic bridge at mm. 16–19. [Waters 2012, paras. 5–6](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.waters.html)
4. **_Nocturnes_: “Nuages” (1897–99), especially mm. 1–28, 64–79, 80ff.** This orchestral comparator prevents a piano-only account. Hepokoski hears five varied rotations rather than mere static ABA: a mutable “clouds” module and a more invariant English-horn signal recur with altered duration, harmony, and orchestration; mm. 64–79 are a bounded pentatonic, dynamically/texturally intensified zone. [James Hepokoski, “Clouds and Circles,” _Dutch Journal of Music Theory_ 15/1 (2010)](https://jameshepokoski.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2010-Clouds-Circles-Debussys-Nuages.pdf)
5. **_Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune_ (1892–94), mm. 1–30, and _Jeux_ (1913) as boundary works.** The _Faune_ opening uses chromatic lead-ins, changed orchestration, and elision to connect varied returns at mm. 11, 21, and 26; it shows that Debussyan flow is not reducible to block-chord stasis. [Waters 2012, paras. 7–10](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.waters.html) _Jeux_ is used only as a later boundary: its fragmentation and frequent tempo/material changes should not be projected backward onto a 1903–10 “cathedral” preset.

### Documentary and contextual corpus

- Debussy’s **3 September 1907 letter to Jacques Durand** on _Rondes de printemps_: music cannot, in essence, be poured into a rigorous traditional form; it consists of “colours and rhythmicized time.” The original is in François Lesure, ed., _Correspondance 1884–1918_ (Hermann, 1993), pp. 230–31; text and translation are quoted and bibliographically located by Hepokoski. [Hepokoski 2010, pp. 1–2](https://jameshepokoski.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/2010-Clouds-Circles-Debussys-Nuages.pdf)
- Debussy’s criticism, collected as **_Monsieur Croche, antidilettante_** (articles largely from 1901 and 1903, revised by Debussy during the 1910s; published 1921). DICTECO explains the complicated publication history: Debussy selected/revised articles but rejected 1914 proofs as formless, so the 1921 book is primary testimony with an editorial caveat. [DICTECO dossier](https://dicteco.huma-num.fr/fr/anthology/26584) A scan is available here: [1921 edition](https://dicteco.huma-num.fr/en/pdffile/60834/raw/claude_debussy__monsieur_.pdf). His comments on Massenet explicitly invoke the neglected “value of silence”; this supports silence as an aesthetic value, not a mechanical command that every phrase must contain a rest. [Debussy, “L’Opéra,” transcribed from the 1921 edition](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Monsieur_Croche/L%E2%80%99Op%C3%A9ra)
- Debussy’s recollection of **Javanese music**, written in 1913, praises its counterpoint and learning from sea, wind, leaves, and small noises. Parker quotes it and reconstructs the 1889 Exposition context, while warning through detailed comparison that _Pagodes_ is a composed Western piano response, not a transcription. [Sylvia Parker, “Claude Debussy’s Gamelan,” _College Music Symposium_ 52 (2012)](https://symposium.music.org/volume-52/articles-1752877057/claude-debussys-gamelan)
- Institutional provenance: the BnF’s Gallica portal collects Debussy autograph manuscripts and establishes an authoritative documentary base, including _La mer_ and _Faune_; it does not presently expose an autograph of “La cathédrale” in the retrieved collection, so measure claims above rely on the first printed Durand score. [BnF, “Manuscrits musicaux de Claude Debussy”](https://gallica.bnf.fr/selections/fr/html/manuscrits-musicaux-de-claude-debussy)

## 2. Historical and aesthetic context

Debussy’s mature work engages several traditions at once rather than simply abolishing tonality. Conservatoire training, Wagnerian continuity, Chopin’s pianism, French concern with timbre and arabesque, modal and pentatonic resources, and encounters with non-European music all remain audible. Waters shows that Debussy can avoid functional progressions while preserving strong continuity through common tones, pattern completion, pedals, and overlapping processes. Jeremy Day-O’Connell similarly places Debussy’s pentatonicism within a longer tonal tradition rather than treating it as an exotic scale pasted onto European harmony. [Day-O’Connell, “Debussy, Pentatonicism, and the Tonal Tradition,” in _The Cambridge History of Western Music Theory_ context/reprint](https://www.skidmore.edu/music/documents/Day-OConnell_Debussy_Pentatonicism.pdf)

The 1889 Exposition matters, especially for _Pagodes_, but the historical setting was colonial spectacle. “Gamelan influence” must not become a license to call equal-tempered black-key pentatonicism authentic Javanese music. Parker distinguishes gamelan tuning, layered practice, and colotomic organization from Debussy’s approximation on piano. The preset should therefore cite Debussy, not market itself as gamelan or pan-Asian sound.

“Planing” itself is shared late-nineteenth-/early-twentieth-century vocabulary: parallel chord movement, modal archaism, organal open intervals, and coloristic succession occur beyond Debussy. What is especially useful here is not ownership of parallel chords, but Debussy’s integration of them with resonance, pedals, registral separation, local collection changes, and temporal/formal transformation.

## 3. Career/style evolution and selected period

- **Early/emergent modernism (c. 1890–99):** _Faune_ and _Nocturnes_ loosen functional syntax and foreground timbre, modal centricity, recurring modules, and elided returns. Yet continuity and tonal reference persist.
- **Mature color-form period (c. 1903–10) — the preset’s proper scope:** _Pagodes_, _Reflets_, “Voiles,” and “La cathédrale” use piano resonance as an orchestral medium. Fixed collections and parallel formations appear, but section identity comes equally from bass/pedal, register, attack pattern, density, dynamics, and return/transformation. “La cathédrale” supplies the architectural/dynamic trajectory; “Pagodes” supplies stratified register and pentatonic resonance; “Reflets” supplies overlap and changing harmonic meaning under retained figures; “Voiles” supplies the exceptional whole-tone limiting case.
- **Late/project-specific modernism (1912–15):** _Jeux_ and the _Études_ intensify fragmentation, discontinuity, interval-specific construction, and technical abstraction. Those are not the default identity of this preset. A later-style Debussy preset would need separate research.

Thus “Planed Cathedral” should represent a **bounded mature-piano/orchestral analogy, 1903–10**, not Debussy’s whole career.

## 4. Persistent traits versus period/work traits

### Persistent or broadly recurring traits (high confidence)

1. **Color and register are structural, not decorative.** Piano voicing, pedal resonance, orchestral doubling, and timbral reassignment alter how material functions. _Nuages_ changes orchestration and expansion with each rotation; _Reflets_ retains upper figuration while the bass changes its meaning.
2. **Pitch organization is plural.** Diatonic/modal centers, pentatonic and whole-tone collections, chromatic links, pedals, triads, open fifths, and altered sonorities coexist. Debussy often moves between collection-types rather than remaining inside one symmetric scale.
3. **Continuity by retention and transformation.** Common tones, pattern completion, motivic lead-ins, pedals, and overlap join regions that may be harmonically remote. Waters’s mm. 16–19 in “La cathédrale” and mm. 31–47/68–74 in “Reflets” are direct evidence.
4. **Non-functional does not mean directionless.** Dynamics, register, density, bass arrival, rhythmic augmentation, and recurrence create approach, disclosure, and withdrawal even where dominant–tonic syntax is weak.
5. **Silence/near-silence and decay have formal weight.** Soft openings and codas, held resonance, thinning, and gaps frame sound. The evidence supports space as value, not arbitrary note deletion.

### Mature-period traits most relevant to this preset (high confidence)

- Parallel open fifths/octaves, triads, or chordal formations used in **bounded spans**, often collection-constrained.
- Wide registral separation and pedal-like basses beneath a middle/high plane.
- Gradual accumulation into thicker, louder, more centered sonority, followed by withdrawal or distant return.
- Pentatonic and modal/diatonic fields more often than an unbroken whole-tone field.
- Pianistic “orchestration”: layers separated by register, attack, note-rate, and dynamic level.

### Work-specific, not universal (high confidence)

- **“Voiles”:** unusually strict whole-tone restriction interrupted by pentatonic material. Waters/Pomeroy call it unique in the _Préludes_ and an exceptional pitch-class experiment, not the Debussy norm. [Waters 2012, paras. 31–32](https://mtosmt.org/issues/mto.12.18.3/mto.12.18.3.waters.html)
- **“La cathédrale”:** programmatic emergence/submergence, open “archaic” intervals, monumental chordal climax, and distant after-image.
- **“Pagodes”:** pentatonic stratification, gong-like low punctuation, and a Western piano response to gamelan.
- **“Nuages”:** mutable rotations around a relatively invariant signal, with orchestral rather than chord-plane identity.
- **_Jeux_:** unusually discontinuous and fragmentary later process.

## 5. Relationship to peers and tradition

### Shared vocabulary

- Parallel triads/fifths and modality belong to a wider French/Russian/European vocabulary and to historical practices Debussy could hear as archaic or non-academic.
- Whole-tone collections were not Debussy’s private invention; Russian repertory and contemporaneous modernism also used them.
- Pentatonicism has a long European tonal history as well as many non-European forms. Day-O’Connell’s historical account directly counters the claim that any pentatonic passage is automatically “exotic.”
- Wagner and Chopin provide precedents for long-range continuity and pianistic resonance; Waters explicitly names both traditions in relation to Debussy’s bridges.

### Distinctive conjunction (not exclusive ownership)

Debussy’s characteristic contribution in this corpus is the **coordination** of parallel formations with timbre/register, pedal resonance, collection shifts, motivic retention, dynamic shaping, and fluid formal overlap. A preset can honestly evoke that conjunction. It cannot claim that parallel chords or a whole-tone scale alone identify Debussy.

## 6. Common caricatures to avoid

1. **“Debussy equals whole tone.” Reject.** “Voiles” is the exceptional limiting case. “La cathédrale” traverses pentatonic, diatonic/modal, and other regions; _Reflets_ retains plagal/tonic hearing; _Pagodes_ is chiefly pentatonic.
2. **“Parallel chords mean random chord shapes moved chromatically.” Reject.** Score examples show collection control, pedals/common tones, registral plans, and formal destinations.
3. **“Impressionism means vague, formless wash.” Reject.** Debussy disliked restrictive inherited formulas, but the works have exact recurrence, sectional pacing, registral design, and dynamics. Waters demonstrates bridging continuity; Hepokoski demonstrates rotational organization.
4. **“No functional cadence means no center or trajectory.” Reject.** Pedals, focal pitches, plagal relations, bass changes, and dynamic/register arcs supply direction.
5. **“Pentatonic equals Javanese gamelan.” Reject.** Equal-tempered piano cannot reproduce gamelan tuning or social/performance systems; _Pagodes_ is an imaginative French piano work in a colonial-era context.
6. **“Cathedral means loud organ chords throughout.” Reject.** The force of mm. 28–41 depends on the soft, separated opening and later withdrawal/distant return.
7. **“Soft and pedaled means muddy.” Reject.** Layers remain differentiated by register, attack, rhythm, and dynamic plane; resonance is controlled.
8. **“All voices must move in exact parallel motion.” Reject.** Debussy regularly combines a moving plane with a held pedal, invariant pitch, independent signal, or changed bass.

### Minimal historically honest preset thesis

A slow monophonic line is set within **widely spaced, mostly pentatonic/modal or diatonic chordal planes**, sometimes moving in parallel but often held against a pedal/common tone. Sustained input and phrase growth may broaden register, density, and dynamics; phrase endings should thin into resonance or silence. A bounded whole-tone episode is permissible, but a continuous whole-tone harmonizer would be a caricature.

### What current “harmony baseline” can and cannot honestly claim

- **Likely representable baseline:** collection-constrained chord choices, open fifth/fourth spacing, parallel voice direction, low pedal/common-tone retention, conservative voice count, long releases.
- **Needs verification rather than assumption:** whether the current engine can preserve chord identity across notes, separate register roles, change state from phrase evidence, or guarantee bounded whole-tone episodes.
- **Known conceptual gaps if absent:** phrase/section memory, gradual density/register/dynamic morphing, independent pedal and plane roles, controlled silence/release, pianistic resonance/orchestral timbre. Without these, ship only a clearly named **static mature-Debussy color approximation**, not “the cathedral rises and sinks.”

## 8. Confidence, disagreements, and gaps

### Confidence by claim

- **High:** score locations and dynamic/registral contrasts in the principal corpus; “Voiles” as exceptional; collection plurality; planing integrated with pedals and voice leading; mature 1903–10 scope.
- **High–medium:** “La cathédrale” as an emergence/withdrawal arc. The score strongly supports it, while exact formal labels differ.
- **Medium:** broad attribution of Debussy’s pentatonic/stratified practice to the 1889/1900 gamelan encounters. The encounter and later comments are documented, but influence is mediated and individual techniques also have European precedents.
- **Medium:** silence as a career-wide compositional invariant. Debussy’s criticism and multiple scores support its value; one remark about Massenet cannot alone establish a universal rule.
- **Low/not claimed:** exact Debussy-authentic playback from a harmony engine; authentic Javanese tuning or performance; a single algorithm covering his whole career.

### Live disagreements

1. **Continuity versus stasis.** Kramer/Wenk traditions emphasize circularity and sonorous stasis; Waters finds strong bridges and continuous transformation. Best resolution: both are work- and passage-dependent. The preset should support local lingering without losing phrase/section trajectory.
2. **Form of “Nuages.”** Conventional readings hear free ABA′; Hepokoski proposes five rotations with an internal telos at mm. 64–79. Both capture audible features. Implementation implication is unchanged: returns vary; they are not literal loops.
3. **Form of “La cathédrale.”** Published analyses differ (arch-form boundaries, thematic sections, proportional schemes). The robust consensus is the audible/textual contrast among remote opening, emergence, full chordal disclosure, withdrawal, return, and coda; avoid encoding disputed exact proportions.
4. **Gamelan influence.** Some accounts overstate direct derivation; Parker argues detailed correspondence in _Pagodes_, while the colonial setting and equal-tempered medium demand caution. Treat it as documented encounter and creative response, not transcription or ownership.
5. **Meaning of parallel chords.** Analysts can label the same formations as independent color, prolonged tonal/plagal harmony, modal succession, or voice-leading elaboration. Do not force one global theoretical reading; retain the observable behaviors (shared contour, collection, pedal/common tone, registral/dynamic function).
