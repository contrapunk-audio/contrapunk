# Research: Preset 01 — Cloister Organum  
## History / Primary-Source Report

## Summary

A historically coherent preset can reference the **late-twelfth- and early-thirteenth-century Notre-Dame organum tradition shared by the repertories associated with Léonin and Pérotin**: liturgical chant retained as a structural tenor, expanded by one or more newly composed voices, with perfect consonances serving as important structural sonorities. It should not blend Léonin’s predominantly two-voice, alternately florid and discant practice with Pérotin’s rhythmically coordinated three- and four-voice writing into a generic “medieval” texture.

The catalog phrase **“open fourths, fifths, and octaves around a chant line” is only partly defensible**. Fourths, fifths, and octaves belong to the documented consonance vocabulary, but Notre-Dame organum is not simply constant parallel interval doubling; the chant normally occupies the lowest tenor, upper voices are melodically and rhythmically active, and non-perfect vertical intervals occur between structural consonances. A reactive MIDI preset may honestly claim a **bounded modern evocation of chant-supported Notre-Dame organum**, not reconstruction, authentic performance, or imitation of either named composer.

---

## 1. Scope and corpus

### 1.1 Referenced period

This report scopes the preset to the **Notre-Dame polyphonic repertory of approximately the later twelfth through early thirteenth centuries**, as transmitted principally in thirteenth-century manuscripts and described retrospectively by the theorist conventionally called Anonymous IV.

The preset does **not** represent:

- ninth-century parallel organum as described in the *Musica enchiriadis* tradition;
- all medieval sacred polyphony;
- later thirteenth-century motets derived from Notre-Dame clausulae;
- conductus as an interchangeable substitute for chant-based organum;
- modern “monastic,” drone, or cinematic-medieval ambience.

### 1.2 Principal musical witnesses

The most substantial consulted witness is Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, **MS Pluteus 29.1**, conventionally **F**. DIAMM describes it as containing 863 compositions and exposes manuscript images through IIIF. Its organization preserves distinct four-, three-, and two-voice repertory groups rather than one homogeneous style. [DIAMM manuscript record](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/924/)

Particularly relevant folios are:

| Repertory item | Florence F location | Evidentiary significance |
|---|---:|---|
| *Viderunt omnes / Notum fecit*, four voices | ff. 1–4 | Monumental four-voice organum traditionally associated with Pérotin |
| *Sederunt principes / Adiuva me*, four voices | ff. 4–7v | Second named Pérotin quadruplum in Anonymous IV’s report |
| *Viderunt omnes / Notum fecit*, two voices | ff. 99–100v | Demonstrates a separate duplum tradition on the same chant |
| *Sederunt / Adiuva me*, two voices | ff. 101–102 | Two-voice counterpart to the four-voice repertory |
| *Alleluia Pascha nostrum*, two voices | ff. 109–109v | Representative chant-based organum duplum |
| Numerous short two-voice clausulae | approximately ff. 147–184v | Evidence for replacement clausulae and repertorial revision |

These locations are taken from the DIAMM contents record, which importantly catalogs the manuscript pieces as **anonymous**. The composer names come from treatise testimony and later scholarly attribution, not from signatures in F. [DIAMM manuscript contents](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/924/)

Other principal witnesses include:

- Wolfenbüttel, Herzog August Bibliothek, **Cod. Guelf. 628 Helmst. (W1)**, a major *Magnus liber organi* source. [DIAMM W1 record](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/870/)
- Wolfenbüttel, Herzog August Bibliothek, **Cod. Guelf. 1099 Helmst. (W2)**, another major thirteenth-century Notre-Dame source. [DIAMM W2 record](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/854/)
- The modern critical *Magnus Liber Organi* series and Mark Everist’s annotated transcriptions of F. Everist cautions that these transcriptions do not replace the full critical edition but are intended as scholarly and performing resources. [DIAMM transcription project](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/resources/music-editions/organa-and-clausulae-ms-i-fl-pluteus-291/)

### 1.3 Treatise corpus

The central near-contemporary narrative source is **Anonymous IV, *De mensuris et discantu***, probably written several decades after the principal activity it describes. The standard editions/translations include:

- Fritz Reckow, *Der Musiktraktat des Anonymus 4*, 2 vols., Beihefte zum Archiv für Musikwissenschaft 4–5 (Wiesbaden: Franz Steiner, 1967), especially vol. 1, pp. 45–46.
- Jeremy Yudkin, *The Music Treatise of Anonymous IV: A New Translation* (Neuhausen-Stuttgart: Hänssler-Verlag, 1985). [Internet Archive catalog](https://archive.org/details/musictreatiseofa0000unse)
- Anonymous IV, Latin/French working edition, with discussion of its description of the *Magnus liber organi* and its reported succession of Parisian masters. [HAL edition](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-04198547v1/file/Anonyme%20IV%20(latin:Fran%C3%A7ais).pdf)

For compositional vocabulary and rhythm, this report also considers the Notre-Dame theoretical tradition represented by *De mensurabili musica*, conventionally attributed to Johannes de Garlandia:

- Erich Reimer, ed., *Johannes de Garlandia: De mensurabili musica*, 2 vols., Beihefte zum Archiv für Musikwissenschaft 10–11 (Wiesbaden: Franz Steiner, 1972). [Critical-edition scan, vol. 1](https://www.examenapium.it/meri/pdf/Reimer1.pdf)
- Stanley H. Birnbaum, trans., *Concerning Measured Music: De mensurabili musica* (Colorado Springs: Colorado College Music Press, 1978). [Internet Archive catalog](https://archive.org/details/concerningmeasur0000joha)

### 1.4 Attribution limit

No surviving autograph by Léonin or Pérotin was identified. F, W1, and W2 are later repertory witnesses, and their musical contents are mostly anonymous. The correct formulation is therefore:

> “Repertory associated by Anonymous IV and subsequent scholarship with Léonin and Pérotin,”

not:

> “scores demonstrably written and copied by Léonin and Pérotin.”

## 2. Historical and aesthetic context

### 2.1 Liturgical enlargement, not free-standing harmonic composition

Anonymous IV reports that a certain **Magister Leoninus** was an excellent maker of organa and made a great book of organum from the Gradual and Antiphoner **“for the amplification of the divine service”**—*pro servitio divino multiplicando*. The report presents polyphony as an enlargement of pre-existing liturgical chant, not as a progression of autonomous chords.

The chant therefore has three simultaneous roles:

1. inherited liturgical material;
2. formal map determining which textual and melodic portions receive polyphony;
3. structural tenor beneath newly composed voice or voices.

This is the strongest historical basis for the preset’s chant-line concept.

Primary passage: Anonymous IV, *De mensuris et discantu*, in Reckow, vol. 1, pp. 45–46; discussed and translated in Edward H. Roesner, “Who ‘Made’ the *Magnus Liber*?”, *Early Music History* 20 (2001), opening discussion and note 1. [Article record](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261127901001061) · [Accessible article PDF](http://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Roesner2001.pdf)

### 2.2 Selective festal practice

The repertory is not evidence that every chant or every service was continuously polyphonic. The great organa elaborate selected responsorial chants, especially Graduals, Alleluias, and Office responsories appropriate to important feasts. Guillaume Gross characterizes the tripla and quadrupla as virtuosic pieces intended for celebration of the **greatest feasts** in the new cathedral. [Gross, “Organum at Notre-Dame,” abstract and bibliographic record](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0961137106000349)

The alternation of polyphonic and unembellished chant portions matters aesthetically. Notre-Dame organum creates expansion and contrast within a liturgical framework; it is not an uninterrupted “open medieval chord pad.”

### 2.3 Cathedral and institutional context

“Cloister” is evocative product language, but the referenced repertory is specifically associated with an **urban cathedral and its clerical and educational institutions**, not securely with secluded monastic practice. “Cloister Organum” should therefore remain an image-title, not historical metadata.

Similarly, “Notre-Dame School” is a useful modern category but can imply a firmer institution, canon, and composer succession than the evidence warrants. The repertory circulated beyond Paris, and its manuscripts were copied later and in several locations.

### 2.4 Notational and rhythmic problem

Notre-Dame notation records pitch and patterned rhythm with greater specificity in discant passages than in sustained-tenor organum purum, whose rhythmic realization remains disputed. Modern equal-tempered MIDI necessarily imposes exact onset, duration, pitch, and voice-assignment decisions that the sources and treatises do not always determine uniquely.

That limitation prevents the preset from claiming historically authentic timing or performance practice.

## 3. Career and repertory evolution

Biographical “career” reconstruction is unusually hazardous here. The report therefore distinguishes **treatise narrative**, **repertory layers**, and **secure biography**.

### 3.1 Léonin-associated layer

Anonymous IV calls Léonin an excellent **organista** and associates him with the making of a *magnus liber organi* based on Gradual and Antiphonal chants. The principal stylistic category linked by scholarship to this layer is **organum duplum**:

- a chant-bearing tenor;
- one added voice, the duplum;
- sustained-tenor **organum purum** where a chant note may be greatly prolonged beneath a florid upper melody;
- measured **discant** where tenor and duplum participate in patterned rhythm;
- alternation governed partly by the chant’s syllabic and melismatic construction.

Asher Yampolsky’s score-based dissertation describes organum duplum as the preponderant Notre-Dame organum type and defines its main texture as a prolonged chant tenor under a newly composed upper line. His analysis also supports hearing organum purum as both modal and “dronal,” rather than merely as arbitrary consonant interval doubling. [Yampolsky, *Melodic Mode in Notre Dame Organum Duplum*, abstract](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/482281/)

**Biographical confidence:** low to medium. Anonymous IV’s testimony is late; identification of the musician with a documented Parisian cleric or poet named Leonius remains debated.

### 3.2 Pérotin-associated revision and enlargement

Anonymous IV reports that **Perotinus Magnus**, praised as an excellent **discantor**, abbreviated or edited the book and made better replacement clausulae or *puncta*. He specifically names:

- the four-voice *Viderunt omnes*;
- the four-voice *Sederunt principes*;
- three-voice pieces including *Alleluia Posui adiutorium* and *Nativitas*.

The F manuscript corroborates the existence and placement of the two four-voice pieces at ff. 1–7v, although F itself labels them anonymously. [DIAMM F record](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/924/)

The Pérotin-associated repertory is not simply “Léonin plus more open intervals.” Its significant development is:

- expansion from two to three or four voices;
- patterned coordination among the upper voices;
- repeated or rhythmically organized tenor segments in discant;
- stronger sectional construction through modal rhythm, repetitions, rests, and cadential articulations;
- revision of inherited organum through substitute clausulae rather than replacement of chant as foundation.

### 3.3 Chronology and named feast dates

*Viderunt omnes* is often connected with Christmas celebrations around 1198 and *Sederunt principes* with St Stephen’s feast in 1199. These datings rely on the intersection of liturgical records, episcopal regulation, and stylistic attribution; Anonymous IV names the works but does not supply those modern calendar datings.

Accordingly:

- **High confidence:** four-voice settings of these chants survive and Anonymous IV associates them with Pérotin.
- **Medium confidence:** composition or performance around the commonly proposed 1198/1199 occasions.
- **Low confidence:** an exact premiere, exact personnel, or one definitive performance version.

### 3.4 Preset’s selected period

The preset should reference the **shared Notre-Dame Magnus-liber tradition**, approximately the Léonin-associated duplum layer through Pérotin-associated clausula revision and selected tripla/quadrupla.

For a bounded reactive implementation, the safest center is:

> **chant-supported organum duplum with occasional additional consonant voices, informed by but not attempting to recreate Pérotin’s composed tripla and quadrupla.**

That is narrower and more coherent than averaging every surviving work attributed to both names.

## 4. Persistent traits versus period-specific traits

### 4.1 Persistent across the selected intersection

The following recur sufficiently across the relevant Notre-Dame repertory to define the preset’s historical center:

1. **Pre-existing liturgical chant as structural material.**
2. **Voice hierarchy:** a tenor derived from chant and one or more added voices.
3. **Temporal enlargement of chant**, especially prolonged tenor tones or repeated tenor segments.
4. **Perfect consonances as structural anchors**, particularly unison, fifth, and octave; the fourth belongs to the medieval consonance discourse but is context-sensitive in multi-voice sonorities.
5. **Cadential convergence** rather than perpetual static spacing.
6. **Sectional contrast:** plainchant versus polyphony, and sustained-tenor versus discant textures.
7. **Modal pitch organization**, not major/minor functional harmony.
8. **Liturgical and rhetorical pacing**, with expansion tied to chant text and feast.

### 4.2 Léonin-associated or duplum-specific traits

- Predominantly two-voice texture.
- Florid duplum above a very slow chant tenor.
- Flexible or disputed rhythm in organum purum.
- Alternation between organum purum, copula-like transitional behavior, and measured discant.
- A relatively exposed relationship between one tenor note and one extended upper-voice span.

These should not be treated as complete descriptions of Pérotin’s quadrupla.

### 4.3 Pérotin-associated traits

- Three- and four-voice monumental works.
- Multiple active upper voices moving at related rates above the tenor.
- Greater dependence on modal rhythmic patterning and coordinated phrase units.
- Repetition and exchange of patterned material among upper voices.
- Replacement clausulae and repertory revision.
- Dense composite sonorities that cannot be reduced to a single fixed interval stack.

These are piece- and layer-specific, not universal properties of all Notre-Dame organum.

### 4.4 Earlier organum traits deliberately excluded

Constant parallel motion at a fourth or fifth is most strongly associated with descriptions of **earlier organum traditions**, including the ninth-century *enchiriadis* complex. That history contributes to the long background of medieval polyphony, but it is not an adequate summary of the Notre-Dame repertory.

The preset must therefore exclude:

- automatic fixed parallel-fourth or parallel-fifth duplication on every note;
- an implication that Léonin and Pérotin invented or principally practiced elementary parallel organum;
- averaging early parallel organum with Pérotin’s quadrupla merely because both are “medieval.”

## 5. Relationship to peers and tradition

### 5.1 Shared vocabulary

Léonin- and Pérotin-associated repertories participate in traditions extending beyond two unique individuals:

- inherited Gregorian and local chant;
- earlier improvised and written polyphony;
- Aquitanian and Parisian experiments with sustained-note polyphony;
- modal rhythm and discant practice;
- collective copying, revision, replacement clausulae, and transmission.

Perfect consonances, chant tenors, and modal organization are therefore **shared historical vocabulary**, not personal signatures.

### 5.2 Documented intersection of the two references

The defensible intersection is not “both liked fourths and fifths.” It is:

1. Anonymous IV places both within the history of the same *Magnus liber* tradition.
2. Pérotin is reported to revise or abbreviate material associated with Léonin and to supply improved clausulae.
3. The major manuscripts preserve different voice-count versions and numerous clausulae around the same chant repertory.
4. Both layers retain chant as the structural point of reference while elaborating it through added voices.
5. Their reported distinction—*organista* versus *discantor*—shows continuity with meaningful specialization, not identical style.

This documented revision relationship is what makes one bounded preset coherent.

### 5.3 Meaningful differences deliberately excluded

The preset should not claim to merge:

- Léonin’s florid two-voice organum-purum timing with Pérotin’s precisely composed multi-upper-voice coordination;
- one-voice-against-tenor improvisatory freedom with four-voice score-specific counterpoint;
- all substitute clausulae with complete organa;
- anonymous layers of F, W1, and W2 into two securely identified personal catalogs.

A static interval generator can evoke only the common structural shell, not these distinctions in full.

### 5.4 Why this is not a generic medieval average

A coherent technique emerges if the preset is bounded to:

> **A slow monophonic line treated as a chant-like tenor, temporally enlarged and answered or crowned by a small number of predominantly upper voices that favor perfect-consonance arrivals while retaining stepwise modal motion and phrase rests.**

This excludes unrelated medieval repertories and captures a documented Notre-Dame relationship: chant foundation, temporal expansion, added polyphony, and structural consonance.

## 6. Common caricatures to avoid

### 6.1 “Medieval music means parallel fifths”

This substitutes an elementary textbook account of early organum for the more differentiated Notre-Dame repertory. Parallel perfect intervals can occur, but constant automatic parallelism is not an adequate Léonin/Pérotin model.

### 6.2 “Only fourths, fifths, and octaves occur”

Late-twelfth- and thirteenth-century writers emphasize perfect consonances, and Ernest Sanders quotes an organum treatise stating that knowledge of fourth, fifth, and octave is indispensable to proper discant. That does not mean every sounding verticality must be one of those intervals. Melodic movement creates other intervals between structural arrivals. [Sanders, “Consonance and Rhythm in the Organum of the 12th and 13th Centuries,” *JAMS* 33/2 (1980), pp. 264–286](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Sanders1980.pdf)

### 6.3 “The chant is surrounded symmetrically”

The chant is normally the **tenor and lowest structural voice** in this repertory. “Around” is acceptable only as loose product language meaning “in relation to.” It should not imply historical upper-and-lower symmetrical harmonization.

### 6.4 “Pérotin simply added voices to Léonin”

Anonymous IV describes revision, abbreviation, replacement clausulae, and excellence in discant. Pérotin’s associated multi-voice repertory changes rhythmic and contrapuntal organization, not merely voice count.

### 6.5 “The composers and their careers are well documented”

Almost everything commonly said about the two composers rests on a small amount of late testimony plus contested archival identification. Manuscript attributions are not composer signatures.

### 6.6 “Cathedral reverberation explains the style”

The architecture is relevant context, but claims that exact note lengths, rhythms, or compositional devices were caused by Notre-Dame’s acoustics require more evidence than the surviving sources provide.

### 6.7 “Cloister equals Notre-Dame”

The repertory is cathedral-centered. The title may evoke sacred enclosure and resonance, but documentation should say “Notre-Dame organum,” not “authentic cloister chant.”

### 6.8 “A MIDI chord stack reconstructs organum”

A reactive harmonizer lacks the liturgical chant source, notation, composed melismas, replacement clausulae, modal rhythmic organization, singer practice, tuning, and manuscript-specific form necessary for reconstruction.

## 7. Primary and near-primary evidence

### 7.1 Anonymous IV on Léonin and Pérotin

Anonymous IV’s famous passage reports, in substance:

- Magister Leoninus was an excellent *organista*;
- he made a great book of organa from Gradual and Antiphonal chants to enlarge divine service;
- the book remained in use until the time of Perotinus Magnus;
- Pérotin, an excellent *discantor*, abbreviated or revised it and made better clausulae or *puncta*;
- Pérotin made the four-voice *Viderunt* and *Sederunt*, along with named three-voice works.

**Citation:** Anonymous IV, *De mensuris et discantu*, ed. Fritz Reckow, *Der Musiktraktat des Anonymus 4*, vol. 1 (Wiesbaden, 1967), pp. 45–46. See Edward H. Roesner, “Who ‘Made’ the *Magnus Liber*?”, *Early Music History* 20 (2001), especially the opening quotation and discussion. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261127901001061) · [PDF](http://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Roesner2001.pdf)

**Interpretive caution:** Roesner questions treating “making” the *Magnus liber* as straightforward sole authorship. The surviving book is better understood as a layered repertory shaped by compilation, transmission, and revision.

### 7.2 Florence F: four-voice works

- *Viderunt omnes*, F ff. 1–4.
- *Sederunt principes*, F ff. 4–7v.

Both are transmitted in four voices at the head of F. Their manuscript presence strongly corroborates Anonymous IV’s identification of a conspicuous quadruplum repertory, while the manuscript’s anonymity prevents treating it as direct autograph evidence. [DIAMM F contents](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/924/)

Mark Everist’s transcription project provides a modern score resource:

- **Fascicle 1:** “Organa, Conductus a4, Clausulae a4 and a3.” [DIAMM edition portal](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/resources/music-editions/organa-and-clausulae-ms-i-fl-pluteus-291/)

For these pieces, manuscript folios are more stable references than modern bar numbers, which vary by editorial rhythmic realization.

### 7.3 Florence F: two-voice counterparts

- *Viderunt omnes*, F ff. 99–100v.
- *Sederunt*, F ff. 101–102.
- *Alleluia Pascha nostrum*, F ff. 109–109v.

These folios show why the repertory cannot be represented by the four-voice monuments alone. F preserves a substantial two-voice chant-based organum corpus alongside its tripla and quadrupla. [DIAMM F contents](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/924/)

### 7.4 Clausula evidence

F’s later two-voice fascicles contain numerous short settings on individual tenor words or syllables—multiple versions of *Dominus*, *In saeculum*, *Nostrum*, *Latus est*, and others. This materially supports a culture of alternate or substitute clausulae rather than a single fixed harmonization for an entire chant. See especially the dense contents listings around F ff. 147–184v. [DIAMM F contents](https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/924/)

### 7.5 Treatise evidence for perfect consonances

Sanders’s survey quotes a twelfth-century organum instruction that a maker of discant must know the fourth, fifth, and octave because proper discant is constructed through them. This supports treating perfect intervals as indispensable structural vocabulary.

**Citation:** Ernest H. Sanders, “Consonance and Rhythm in the Organum of the 12th and 13th Centuries,” *Journal of the American Musicological Society* 33, no. 2 (Summer 1980): 264–286. [Accessible PDF](https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Sanders1980.pdf)

It does **not** establish that every note in Notre-Dame organum is harmonized by a static perfect interval.

### 7.6 Score-based scholarship on organum duplum

Yampolsky’s corpus analysis finds that modal organization, pitch affinities, and consonance all help explain duplum construction. The result argues against reducing the upper voice to one constant transposition of the tenor.

**Citation:** Asher Vijay Yampolsky, *Melodic Mode in Notre Dame Organum Duplum*, PhD diss., University of Southampton, 2020, 294 pp.; especially chapters 1 and 5 as summarized in the institutional abstract. [University record](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/482281/)

### 7.7 Cultural function of tripla and quadrupla

Gross treats the elaboration of tripla and quadrupla as a culturally situated, virtuosic practice for major feasts.

**Citation:** Guillaume Gross, “Organum at Notre-Dame in the Twelfth and Thirteenth Centuries: Rhetoric in Words and Music,” *Plainsong and Medieval Music* 15, no. 2 (2006): 87–108. [DOI and abstract](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0961137106000349)

## 8. Evaluation of the catalog promise

### Promise

> “Open fourths, fifths, and octaves around a chant line.”

### 8.1 Historically defensible components

**Defensible with qualification:**

- **Chant line:** strongly defensible. The organum repertory is structurally based on liturgical chant.
- **Open fifths and octaves:** defensible as prominent structural consonances, especially for beginnings, arrivals, and cadential stability.
- **Fourths:** defensible as part of medieval consonance theory and two-voice discant vocabulary, but treatment depends on voice context and texture.
- **Slow held line:** defensible as an evocation of sustained-tenor organum purum.
- **Sparse phrase rests:** defensible as a modern analogue for sectional articulation and alternation with chant.

### 8.2 Misleading implications

The promise becomes misleading if implemented or described as:

- constant parallel fourths, fifths, and octaves;
- a three-note “power-chord” stack on every MIDI input;
- symmetric voices both above and below a central chant;
- chord-by-chord harmonization of a freely improvised modern melody;
- a model equally representative of Léonin’s duplum and Pérotin’s quadrupla;
- evidence that only perfect intervals sounded.

### 8.3 Recommended historical wording

A safer result description is:

> **“A slow chant-like line is prolonged beneath sparse organal voices that favor open fifths and octaves, with occasional fourths and modal stepwise motion.”**

If product brevity requires the original wording, supporting copy should add:

> **“A bounded modern evocation of Notre-Dame organum, not a reconstruction of Léonin or Pérotin.”**

## 9. What a modern reactive MIDI approximation must not claim

The preset must not claim:

1. **Authentic reconstruction.** It does not read a historical chant, manuscript, or liturgical occasion.
2. **Composer imitation.** It cannot recreate Léonin’s composed duplum melodies or Pérotin’s score-specific upper-voice counterpoint.
3. **Authentic rhythm.** Organum-purum rhythm and editorial realization remain contested; reactive MIDI imposes modern timing.
4. **Authentic tuning.** Equal-tempered MIDI is not evidence for medieval interval tuning or vocal intonation.
5. **Authentic voice disposition.** The player’s line may function as a practical input proxy, not necessarily as a historically transmitted tenor.
6. **Authentic form.** True organa alternate chant, organum purum, discant, clausulae, and cadences according to specific liturgical melodies and texts.
7. **Authentic venue or ensemble.** Reverb, organ patches, choirs, or “cloister” imagery do not establish Notre-Dame performance conditions.
8. **A complete Léonin–Pérotin synthesis.** The implementation captures only their repertories’ documented intersection.

An honest approximation statement is:

> **“Cloister Organum uses slow input as a chant-like tenor and adds bounded, predominantly upper voices favoring structural perfect consonances. It evokes one shared feature of the Notre-Dame organum repertory; it does not reconstruct medieval scores, rhythm, tuning, liturgy, or the personal styles of Léonin and Pérotin.”**

## 10. Confidence and gaps

### 10.1 Claim-level confidence

| Claim | Confidence | Basis / limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Notre-Dame organum elaborates pre-existing liturgical chant | **High** | Treatise testimony, repertory contents, and surviving tenor texts |
| Organum duplum is central to the repertory | **High** | Large surviving corpus in F/W1/W2 and modern score-based scholarship |
| Perfect fourths, fifths, and octaves are important theoretical consonances | **High** | Contemporary/near-contemporary treatise tradition and Sanders’s source study |
| Constant perfect-interval parallelism describes Notre-Dame organum | **High confidence that this is false** | Scores and scholarship show differentiated upper melodies, sectional styles, and other verticalities |
| Léonin made a great book of organum and Pérotin revised it | **Medium** | Explicit Anonymous IV report, but written decades later and challenged as a literal authorship history |
| F’s complete two-voice corpus is substantially Léonin’s personal work | **Low to medium** | Conventional association, not manuscript attribution or autograph proof |
| *Viderunt* and *Sederunt* quadrupla are by Pérotin | **Medium to high** | Named by Anonymous IV and matched to exceptional surviving four-voice works; still not autographs |
| Exact performances occurred at Christmas 1198 and St Stephen 1199 | **Medium** | Plausible documentary reconstruction, not stated in the musical manuscripts or Anonymous IV |
| Léonin and Pérotin had a documented teacher–student relationship | **Low** | Repertory succession is documented; personal pedagogy is not securely established |
| Cathedral acoustics directly determined the style | **Low** | Plausible context but insufficient for causal claims |
| Equal-tempered open stacks reproduce medieval consonance | **Low** | Modern technical approximation, not historical tuning evidence |

### 10.2 Unresolved gaps

1. **Authorship boundaries:** The exact share of the surviving *Magnus liber* attributable to either named figure remains unresolved.
2. **Biography:** Léonin’s and Pérotin’s identities, offices, dates, and career sequences are only partially reconstructable.
3. **Rhythm of organum purum:** Treatises and notation permit competing modern realizations.
4. **Tuning and vocal execution:** Manuscripts do not prescribe a single modern reproducible temperament, ensemble size, or timbre.
5. **Fourth treatment:** “Fourth” cannot safely become an unrestricted lowest-voice sonority rule across all multi-voice textures.
6. **Modern measure numbers:** Editorial bar numbering varies. Manuscript folios and named sections are the more reliable traceability references.
7. **Liturgy-to-interaction translation:** A player-generated melody is not automatically equivalent to a historically authorized chant tenor.

### 10.3 Recommended validation before implementation

For parent synthesis, compare any proposed interval algorithm against short passages from:

- F ff. 99–100v, two-voice *Viderunt omnes*;
- F ff. 109–109v, two-voice *Alleluia Pascha nostrum*;
- F ff. 1–4, four-voice *Viderunt omnes*;
- F ff. 4–7v, four-voice *Sederunt principes*.

The test is not exact note reproduction. It is whether the implementation preserves the historically bounded thesis—**slow structural tenor, independently shaped upper motion, selective perfect-consonance arrivals, and cadential convergence**—rather than emitting permanent parallel stacks.

## Sources

### Kept

- **Anonymous IV, *De mensuris et discantu***, ed. Fritz Reckow, *Der Musiktraktat des Anonymus 4*, vol. 1, pp. 45–46 — principal near-contemporary testimony naming Léonin, Pérotin, the *Magnus liber*, and named multi-voice works.
- **Edward H. Roesner, “Who ‘Made’ the *Magnus Liber*?”** ([DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0261127901001061); [PDF](http://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Roesner2001.pdf)) — authoritative critique of simplistic single-composer authorship and careful translation of Anonymous IV.
- **DIAMM, Florence MS Pluteus 29.1** (https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/924/) — institutional manuscript catalog, folio references, voice counts, and facsimile access.
- **DIAMM, W1** (https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/870/) and **W2** (https://www.diamm.ac.uk/sources/854/) — authoritative records for the other principal Notre-Dame witnesses.
- **Mark Everist/DIAMM, transcriptions from F** (https://www.diamm.ac.uk/resources/music-editions/organa-and-clausulae-ms-i-fl-pluteus-291/) — current score-based scholarly resource with explicit editorial limitations.
- **Ernest H. Sanders, “Consonance and Rhythm in the Organum of the 12th and 13th Centuries”** (https://www.examenapium.it/cs/biblio/Sanders1980.pdf) — source-based evidence for consonance vocabulary and rhythmic practice.
- **Asher Vijay Yampolsky, *Melodic Mode in Notre Dame Organum Duplum*** (https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/482281/) — institutional, corpus-based analysis of modal and dronal organization.
- **Guillaume Gross, “Organum at Notre-Dame…”** (https://doi.org/10.1017/S0961137106000349) — scholarly account of tripla/quadrupla as virtuosic festal elaboration.
- **Johannes de Garlandia, *De mensurabili musica*** ([critical edition](https://www.examenapium.it/meri/pdf/Reimer1.pdf); [translation catalog](https://archive.org/details/concerningmeasur0000joha)) — central theoretical witness for measured polyphony and rhythmic modes.

### Dropped

- **Wikipedia summaries of Léonin, Pérotin, Anonymous IV, and the *Magnus liber*** — useful discovery aids but unnecessary where primary editions and institutional catalogs are available.
- **Scribd/PDFCoffee classroom analyses of *Alleluia Pascha nostrum*** — unclear provenance and weaker authority than manuscripts, critical editions, and university scholarship.
- **General music-appreciation pages describing organum as parallel fourths or fifths** — conflate early parallel organum with the later Notre-Dame repertory.
- **Blogs and unsourced listening guides to *Viderunt* or *Sederunt*** — excluded where claims could be grounded in F, Anonymous IV, or peer-reviewed scholarship.
- **Modern acoustical or architectural reconstructions** — potentially valuable for a separate performance report, but insufficient as primary evidence for compositional rules.

---

## Historical decision

**Suitable only as a bounded approximation.**

The two named references have a documented and coherent intersection in the Notre-Dame *Magnus liber* tradition, but the current catalog wording needs qualification. Preserve chant foundation, temporal expansion, selective perfect-consonance anchoring, upper-voice hierarchy, and phrase-level cadence. Reject constant parallel stacks and any claim of authentic Léonin/Pérotin reconstruction.
